## Q1. Attempt a Comparative Examination of the Views of Marx and Weber on ‘Power’ (2011) — 30 Marks

### What is Power?

**Power** means the capacity of a person or group to influence the behaviour of others and get its will carried out. **Max Weber** defines it as the ability to impose one’s will even against resistance. Thus, power is a central concept of politics, just as money is central to economics.

### Marx and Weber on Power — 8 Dimensions

**1. Source of Power:**  
For **Marx**, the main source of power is **economic ownership**, especially control over the means of production. For **Weber**, economic resources are only one source; power may also arise from political position, tradition, charisma and legal authority. Thus Weber gives a broader understanding of power.

**2. Nature of Social Power:**  
Marx mainly understands power through **class relations** between the bourgeoisie and proletariat. Those who control economic resources are able to dominate those who do not. Weber does not reduce all power relations to the conflict between economic classes.

**3. Concentration of Power:**  
For Marxists, the nature of **money and power is similar**—both tend to become concentrated in a few hands. Therefore, capitalist inequality also produces unequal political power. Weber accepts inequality of power but does not see economic concentration as its only explanation.

**4. View of the State:**  
Marx sees the capitalist state mainly as an **instrument of the dominant class**, protecting bourgeois property and interests. Weber gives the state greater autonomy and sees it as an organisation capable of exercising power through its own institutions and bureaucracy.

**5. Coercion and Legitimacy:**  
Marx gives greater importance to the **coercive character of class rule**, where the state helps maintain the existing economic order. Weber argues that stable rule cannot depend only on naked force; power generally becomes durable when people consider it **legitimate**.

**6. Authority:**  
Marx explains political authority mainly in relation to the underlying **material and class structure** of society. Weber distinguishes legitimate authority into **traditional, charismatic and rational-legal authority**. Thus, Weber explains why people may obey even without direct economic coercion.

**7. Explanation of Society:**  
Marx gives primary importance to the **economic base**; the class controlling the means of production also influences political and social institutions. Weber criticises such a largely one-sided explanation and gives importance to several independent social and political factors in shaping power.

**8. Future of Power:**  
Marx believes that abolition of private property and class divisions would ultimately remove the basis of **class domination**, leading towards a classless society. Weber is more doubtful that power hierarchies can disappear; even after capitalism, bureaucracy and other forms of authority may continue.

### Synthesis

Marx and Weber therefore need not be seen as completely opposed. **Marx explains who gains power through control of economic resources**, while **Weber explains how that power is organised, legitimised and exercised through institutions and authority**. Together, they provide a more complete understanding of modern power relations.

A good contemporary example is the **digital economy**. Marx helps us understand the power that large corporations gain through concentration of capital and economic resources, while Weber helps explain the independent role of governments, laws and bureaucratic regulators in controlling such corporations.

### Conclusion

**Marx locates the foundation of power mainly in economic class relations, whereas Weber provides a broader, multidimensional understanding of power and legitimate authority.**  
Hence, Marx explains the **material basis of power**, while Weber explains its **institutional and legitimate forms**.

## Q2. Examine the Nature and Meaning of Power — 2020 (15 Marks)

### What is Power?

**Power** is the capacity of a person or group to influence the behaviour and choices of others. **Max Weber** understood it as the ability to carry out one’s will even against resistance. Thus, power is central to understanding political and social relationships.

### Nature and Meaning of Power — Different Dimensions

**1. Power as Decision-making:**  
At its most visible level, power means the ability to **take decisions and make others follow them**. Steven Lukes identifies this as the first dimension of power. Here, power can be directly observed through decisions and conflicts.

**2. Power as Agenda-setting:**  
Power also lies in deciding **which issues will be discussed and which will remain outside debate**. Marxist analysis shows how economically dominant groups can influence the political agenda. Thus, power may operate even before an actual decision is taken.

**3. Power as Thought Control:**  
The deepest form of power operates by shaping people's **beliefs, preferences and understanding of their interests**. Gramsci explains this through **hegemony**, where dominant ideas come to appear natural or common sense. Power therefore works through consent as well as coercion.

**4. Power is Diffused:**  
**Foucault** argues that power is not located only in the state or the propertied class. It flows throughout society through institutions, social relations and everyday practices. Therefore, power has **no single centre or single direction**.

**5. Power and Knowledge:**  
For Foucault, power also works through **knowledge and discourse**, which define what society considers normal or acceptable. Institutions can shape behaviour through information, observation and discipline. Hence, power may operate without continuous physical force.

**6. Power can be Productive:**  
Power is not always merely negative or repressive. Foucault argues that it can create **order, identities and disciplined behaviour**, while Hannah Arendt sees power as the enhanced capacity produced by **collective action**. Thus, power can mean both **“power over” and “power to.”**

### Synthesis

Hence, power has a **multidimensional nature**. It may operate through direct decisions, control of agendas, ideas, knowledge, institutions or collective action; therefore, it cannot be reduced only to physical coercion or control by the state.

### Relevant Example

**CCTV-based traffic regulation** shows Foucault’s idea of disciplinary power. Knowledge produced through surveillance allows authorities to regulate behaviour, while people gradually begin to follow traffic rules even without the constant physical presence of police.

### Conclusion

Power therefore operates through both **visible and invisible mechanisms**, ranging from coercion to consent and knowledge.  
Its study explains not merely **who rules**, but also **how behaviour, choices and beliefs themselves are shaped**.

## Q3. Distinguish between Power and Authority — 2015 (15 Marks)

### Meaning of Power

**Power** is the capacity of a person or group to influence the behaviour of others and get its will carried out. **Max Weber** defines power as the ability to achieve one’s will even against resistance. Therefore, power may exist even without the consent of those over whom it is exercised.

### Meaning of Authority

**Authority is legitimate power**—power that people recognise as rightful and acceptable. People obey authority not merely because they are forced, but because they believe that the command has a valid basis. Weber identifies **traditional, charismatic and rational-legal authority**.

### Power and Authority — 6 Dimensions of Difference

**1. Legitimacy:**  
Power does not necessarily require **legitimacy**; a person may compel others through force or influence. Authority, however, exists only when power is considered legitimate. Thus, **legitimacy converts power into authority**.

**2. Basis of Obedience:**  
Under power, people may obey because of **fear, pressure or dependence**. Under authority, obedience largely comes from acceptance that the ruler or institution has the **right to command**. Hence authority normally creates more willing compliance.

**3. Nature of Relationship:**  
Power often represents **“power over”**, where one actor influences or controls another even against resistance. Authority creates a recognised relationship between those who command and those who obey. Therefore, authority is a more institutionalised form of power.

**4. Source:**  
Power can arise from different sources such as **economic resources, political position, knowledge or social influence**. Authority requires an accepted basis of legitimacy. For Weber, this may come from **tradition, charisma or rational-legal rules**.

**5. Stability:**  
Mere power dependent mainly upon coercion can generate **resistance** and may be difficult to sustain. Authority is relatively more stable because people accept its exercise as rightful. Thus, legitimacy reduces dependence upon continuous coercion.

**6. Scope:**  
Power is a **broader concept** and exists throughout social relations, institutions and society. Authority is narrower because it refers only to those forms of power that have acquired legitimacy. Therefore, **all authority involves power, but all power is not authority**.

### Synthesis

Power and authority are therefore closely connected rather than completely separate. A political system may possess the capacity to enforce decisions, but when its exercise of power receives **public acceptance and legitimacy**, that power becomes authority and governance becomes more stable.

### Relevant Example

A **government collecting taxes according to constitutionally established law** exercises rational-legal authority because citizens recognise its legal right to collect them. In contrast, an extortion group may force people to pay money and therefore possess **power**, but it lacks legitimate **authority**.

### Conclusion

**Power explains the capacity to secure obedience, while authority explains the legitimate right to secure that obedience.**  
Hence, legitimacy is the crucial bridge that transforms **naked power into accepted authority**.

## Q4. Explain the Relationship between Power, Authority and Legitimacy — 2018 (15 Marks)

### Meaning of Power, Authority and Legitimacy

**Power** is the capacity of a person or institution to influence others and get its decisions followed, even against resistance. **Authority** is power that is accepted as rightful by those who obey it. **Legitimacy** is the belief or acceptance that such exercise of power has a valid right to command.

### Relationship between Power, Authority and Legitimacy

**1. Power is the starting point:**  
Power provides the **capacity to make and enforce decisions**, but power alone does not give a right to rule. A ruler may possess coercive power even when people do not accept it. Therefore, power can exist without authority.

**2. Legitimacy converts Power into Authority:**  
When people believe that the exercise of power is **rightful and acceptable**, power acquires legitimacy. Such legitimate power becomes **authority**. Hence, the basic relationship can be expressed as **Power + Legitimacy = Authority**.

**3. Authority makes Power stable:**  
Power based mainly on force requires continuous coercion and can create resistance. Authority produces more willing obedience because people recognise the ruler's **right to command**. Thus, legitimacy makes the exercise of power more stable and effective.

**4. Legitimacy provides the basis of Authority:**  
According to **Max Weber**, authority may derive legitimacy from **tradition, charisma or rational-legal rules**. People therefore obey not simply because the ruler is powerful, but because they accept the source of that authority as valid.

**5. Loss of Legitimacy weakens Authority:**  
If people stop accepting a government or institution as rightful, its **authority declines**, even though it may still possess physical power. It may then increasingly depend on coercion to secure obedience. Thus, a legitimacy crisis can become a **crisis of governability**.

**6. Consent continuously sustains the relationship:**  
In a democracy, legitimacy is maintained through mechanisms such as **free and fair elections, rights, independent judiciary and freedom of the press**. These generate public acceptance and convert state power into recognised authority. Therefore, legitimacy continuously connects citizens with political power.

### Relevant Example

A **democratic government elected through accepted constitutional procedures** has the power to make and enforce laws. Because citizens broadly recognise the constitution and electoral process as valid, this power receives **legitimacy** and becomes **legal-rational authority**.

### Conclusion

Thus, **power is the capacity to rule, legitimacy is the acceptance of that rule, and authority is legitimate power**.  
Stable government therefore depends not merely upon possession of power, but upon its continuous **legitimation and acceptance**.

## Q5. Discuss the “Crisis of Legitimacy” in Capitalist Societies — 2015 (10 Marks)

### What is Legitimacy?

**Legitimacy** means public acceptance that a ruler, law or political system has a valid **right to exercise power**. For Weber, legitimacy converts mere power into **authority** because people obey not only due to force but because they accept the rule as rightful.

### Why is there a Crisis of Legitimacy in Capitalist Societies?

**1. Conflict between Capitalism and Welfare:**  
**Habermas** argues that capitalist democracies combine two conflicting demands—**private profit and social welfare**. Governments must satisfy citizens through welfare while depending on a capitalist economy for resources. This basic contradiction creates pressure on the legitimacy of the state.

**2. Rising Demands vs Limited Capacity:**  
Democracy continuously creates demands for jobs, welfare and social security, but the state has **limited resources** to satisfy them. When public expectations exceed government capacity, confidence in institutions begins to decline. **Anthony King** describes this situation as **“government overload.”**

**3. Welfare Rollback Creates Resentment:**  
During economic crisis, governments may reduce spending, welfare benefits or fiscal deficits to protect economic stability. Such measures can create dissatisfaction among citizens who had come to expect social protection. This can produce protests, declining trust and ultimately a **legitimation crisis**.

### Synthesis

Thus, the crisis is not simply an economic crisis; it becomes political when the state is unable to balance **market requirements with popular expectations**. Legitimacy can be strengthened through democratic participation, rule of law and continuous engagement between government and citizens.

### Relevant Example

**Greece during its economic and debt crisis** is a suitable example. Cuts in welfare and public expenditure generated public resentment and protests, showing how economic adjustment can also weaken confidence in political institutions.

### Conclusion

The **crisis of legitimacy** arises when capitalist democracies fail to reconcile **private profit with public welfare expectations**.  
Hence, durable legitimacy requires not only economic performance but also **public consent and democratic responsiveness**.

## Q6. Distinguish between Legitimacy and Hegemony — 2012 (15 Marks)

### Meaning of Legitimacy and Hegemony

**Legitimacy** means the acceptance of a ruler, institution or political system as having a **rightful claim to rule**. For Weber, legitimacy converts mere power into authority and may rest on **tradition, charisma or rational-legal rules**.

**Hegemony**, associated with **Antonio Gramsci**, means domination maintained mainly through **ideas, culture and manufactured consent** rather than continuous coercion. The dominant group makes its values appear natural or as society’s “common sense.”

### Legitimacy and Hegemony — 6 Dimensions of Difference

**1. Basic Meaning:**  
Legitimacy asks whether people consider the exercise of power **rightful and acceptable**. Hegemony explains how a dominant group maintains its position by shaping people’s beliefs and values. Thus, legitimacy concerns acceptance, while hegemony concerns deeper ideological domination.

**2. Basis:**  
Legitimacy may arise from **tradition, personal charisma or established legal procedures**, according to Weber. Hegemony arises mainly through **cultural and ideological leadership**. It makes the worldview of the dominant group appear normal and universal.

**3. Mode of Operation:**  
Legitimacy usually works through recognised political institutions, laws and accepted authority. Hegemony works more indirectly through **family, schools, religion, media and civil society**. Therefore, hegemony often operates before people consciously question political authority.

**4. Nature of Consent:**  
In legitimacy, citizens consciously recognise that an institution or ruler has a valid right to command. In hegemony, consent may be **manufactured**, as subordinate groups begin to accept dominant values as their own. Gramsci therefore gives special importance to control over ideas.

**5. Visibility:**  
Legitimacy is relatively more **visible and formal**, expressed through constitutions, elections or recognised customs. Hegemony is generally **subtle and less visible**, because domination becomes part of everyday culture and common sense.

**6. Relation with Coercion:**  
A legitimate system needs less continuous coercion because people broadly accept its right to rule. In Gramsci’s analysis, hegemony forms the **first line of domination through civil society**, while coercive state power becomes more important when ideological consent fails.

### Synthesis

Legitimacy and hegemony are different but closely connected. **Hegemony can help produce legitimacy** by shaping beliefs in favour of the existing order, while legitimacy gives political authority an accepted right to rule. Therefore, modern power often survives through a combination of **formal acceptance and deeper cultural consent**.

### Relevant Example

In a **capitalist democracy**, an elected government derives **legitimacy** from elections, law and constitutional procedures. At the same time, capitalist values such as consumerism may gain **hegemony** through media, education and popular culture, making the existing economic order appear natural.

### Conclusion

**Legitimacy explains why authority is accepted as rightful, whereas hegemony explains how dominant ideas shape that acceptance.**  
Thus, legitimacy works mainly at the level of **political authority**, while hegemony works more deeply through **culture and consciousness**.

## Q7. Examine the Conditions Required for the Maintenance of Legitimacy in Modern Societies — 2014 (20 Marks)

### What is Legitimacy?

**Legitimacy** means the acceptance of a state, law or political authority as having a **rightful claim to rule**. It makes people obey not merely because of force, but because they consider the exercise of power valid and acceptable. Thus, legitimacy converts power into stable authority.

**Max Weber** identifies traditional, charismatic and legal-rational sources of legitimacy. In modern societies, **legal-rational legitimacy** becomes especially important because authority is expected to operate through established laws and institutions rather than personal will.

### Conditions Required for Maintaining Legitimacy

**1. Rule of Law:**  
The first condition is that both rulers and citizens remain subject to **known and impartial laws**. Arbitrary exercise of power weakens public trust, whereas predictable legal procedures strengthen acceptance of institutions. Hence, rule of law forms the foundation of legal-rational legitimacy.

**2. Democratic Participation and Transparency:**  
Citizens must have opportunities to participate through **elections, representation, free press and public discussion**. **Habermas** argues that legitimacy grows when free and equal citizens participate in public deliberation. People are more likely to accept laws when they see themselves as participants in making them.

**3. Effective Governance:**  
Legitimacy also depends upon the state’s ability to provide **security, public goods and effective administration**. A government that repeatedly fails to meet basic public expectations may lose confidence even if it is legally constituted. Thus, legitimacy requires both lawful authority and satisfactory performance.

**4. Social Justice:**  
Modern legitimacy cannot survive with extreme inequality and exclusion. Drawing on **Rawls**, political institutions must protect fair opportunity and ensure that weaker sections are not permanently disadvantaged. Social justice therefore gives an ethical basis to political authority.

**5. Recognition of Diversity:**  
Modern societies are culturally and socially plural, so legitimacy requires accommodation of different identities and interests. **Charles Taylor** stresses recognition and equitable participation of diverse groups. A state that respects diversity creates a stronger sense of belonging and acceptance.

### Criticism

**Gramsci** questions whether legitimacy always represents genuine consent. He argues that dominant groups may **manufacture consent** through institutions of civil society, making existing power relations appear natural. Thus, apparent legitimacy may sometimes hide deeper domination.

**Habermas** shows that legitimacy can decline when the state cannot meet growing social expectations. In capitalist democracies, demands for welfare may clash with the requirements of private profit, producing a **legitimation crisis**. Therefore, formal democratic institutions alone cannot guarantee legitimacy.

**Foucault** further suggests that legitimacy may also be shaped through **discourses, knowledge and governed mentality**. People may accept certain forms of authority because social norms have already shaped what they consider normal. Hence, legitimacy should not always be treated as completely free and conscious consent.

### Synthesis

Thus, legitimacy in modern societies is not maintained by law alone. It requires a continuous combination of **rule of law, participation, good governance, social justice, recognition and genuine public consent**, because legitimacy must be repeatedly renewed as society changes.

### Relevant Example

**India’s constitutional democracy** illustrates this combination through elections, Fundamental Rights, judicial review, welfare programmes and special arrangements for diverse communities. These mechanisms seek to combine legal authority, participation, social justice and recognition within one political framework.

### Conclusion

Legitimacy in modern societies depends on whether citizens see political authority as **lawful, responsive, fair and inclusive**.  
Therefore, durable legitimacy rests less on coercion and more on **continuous consent, participation and effective governance**.

## Q8. “Power is never a property of individual; it belongs to a group and remains in existence only so long as the groups keep together.” — Hannah Arendt

### Background of Hannah Arendt

**Hannah Arendt** was a 20th-century political thinker who studied **totalitarianism, freedom, action and power**. In works like *The Human Condition* and *On Violence*, she rejected the idea that power simply means domination by rulers. Instead, she developed a **constructive and collective view of power**.

### Why did Hannah Arendt give this statement?

Arendt opposed the traditional idea of power as something **possessed by an individual and exercised over others**. For her, real political power emerges when people come together, speak and act for a common purpose. Therefore, power depends on collective support rather than individual strength.

She also wanted to distinguish **power from violence**. A ruler may possess weapons and use force, but this does not necessarily mean genuine power. When people withdraw their support, collective power disappears and the ruler may increasingly depend upon violence.

### Hannah Arendt’s Ideas on Power

**1. Power as Collective Action:**  
Arendt sees power as the capacity of people to **act in concert**. It belongs to a group and survives only while members continue to cooperate. Thus, power is created through participation, agreement and common action.

**2. “Power to” rather than “Power over”:**  
Her conception is not mainly about domination or a command-obedience relationship. Power means **empowerment**—an increased capacity created when individuals act together. Hence, political power can be constructive rather than merely coercive.

**3. Power and Violence are Different:**  
For Arendt, power belongs to the **public realm and collective support**, whereas violence relies on instruments of force. Violence may destroy power but cannot itself create genuine power. Therefore, greater reliance on violence can indicate weakness of underlying political power.

### Criticism

**1. Individual leadership also matters:**  
Arendt may overstate the claim that power exists only in groups. History shows that determined individuals such as **Gandhi or Nelson Mandela** can inspire political change and mobilise others. Thus, individual agency may also be an important source of political influence.

**2. Collective action is not always constructive:**  
A group acting together does not automatically produce democratic or positive power. Collective mobilisation can also turn into **mob violence or social conflict**. Therefore, group unity by itself cannot be treated as inherently desirable.

**3. Foucault gives a broader view:**  
Arendt largely focuses on power generated through collective public action. **Foucault**, however, shows that power also flows through everyday institutions, knowledge, norms and social relations. Hence, power may exist even outside organised collective action.

### Synthesis

Arendt’s major contribution is that she shifts attention from **domination to collective capacity**. Even if individuals, institutions and social structures also possess influence, durable political power generally becomes stronger when it is supported by **participation, consent and collective action**.

### Relevant Example

The **Indian freedom movement** illustrates Arendt’s idea well. Gandhi alone did not possess political power in the conventional sense; his strength grew when millions participated in movements such as **Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience**. Collective action transformed individual leadership into political power.

### Conclusion

Arendt therefore redefines power as something **created among people rather than possessed over people**.  
Her theory highlights that durable political power rests fundamentally on **collective action and continuing public support**.

## Q9. Foucault’s Concept of Power — 2023 (10 Marks)

### What is Power?

**Power** generally means the capacity to influence the behaviour and choices of others. Traditional theories often locate power in the **state, rulers or dominant classes**. Foucault, however, sees power as a network of relations spread throughout society rather than something possessed by one centre.

### Foucault’s Ideas on Power

**1. Power is everywhere and multidirectional:**  
Foucault argues that power has **no single source or direction**; it flows through society like blood through capillaries. It exists in families, schools, workplaces, hospitals and other social relations. Therefore, people are not only subjected to power but also exercise power over others.

**2. Power–Knowledge relationship:**  
For Foucault, **knowledge and power are inseparable** because knowledge helps define what society considers normal, true or acceptable. Institutions use such knowledge to classify and regulate individuals. Thus, control over knowledge and discourse becomes an important form of power.

**3. Disciplinary and productive power:**  
Modern power does not operate only through punishment or force; it works through **surveillance, discipline and normalization**. People gradually regulate their own behaviour, producing what Foucault calls a governed mentality. Power is therefore not only repressive but also **productive**, because it creates identities, behaviour and social order.

### Criticism

Foucault’s idea that power exists **everywhere** creates a problem of political agency. If every institution, discourse and relationship contains power, it becomes difficult to identify who exactly dominates and how meaningful resistance can occur. Foucault responds that **where there is power, there is also resistance**.

### Synthesis

Foucault broadens the study of power beyond the **state and economic class** to everyday social relations. His approach therefore complements traditional theories by explaining the subtle ways in which modern societies shape behaviour through knowledge, norms and institutions.

### Relevant Example

**CCTV-based traffic regulation** illustrates disciplinary power. Surveillance provides knowledge about behaviour, while repeated monitoring encourages people to follow traffic rules even when police are not physically present.

### Conclusion

Foucault transforms power from a **top-down possession into a diffuse and relational process**.  
His key contribution is showing how **knowledge, surveillance and social norms** quietly shape modern individuals and society.

## Q10. Linkage between Power and Hegemony — 2024 (10 Marks)

### What is Power?

**Power** means the capacity to influence the behaviour, choices or actions of others. It may operate through force, institutions, economic control or ideas. Thus, power is not only about commanding people but also about shaping how they think and act.

### Linkage between Power and Hegemony

**1. Hegemony is a form of Power:**  
For **Gramsci**, hegemony is power exercised mainly through **consent rather than coercion**. The dominant class maintains control by making its ideas and values appear natural and acceptable. Thus, hegemony represents the ideological side of power.

**2. Power works through Civil Society:**  
Power is not exercised only through the state, police or law. Institutions such as **family, schools, churches and media** shape social beliefs and produce consent. Gramsci therefore sees civil society as the main site where hegemonic power operates.

**3. Consent reduces need for Coercion:**  
When hegemonic ideas are accepted by subordinate groups, rulers do not need continuous physical force. However, if consent breaks down, the state may rely more on **coercive power**. Hence, political rule combines both **consent and coercion**.

**4. Power can also challenge Hegemony:**  
Hegemony is never permanent because subordinate groups can create **counter-hegemony**. Gramsci’s “war of position” involves challenging dominant ideas through culture, intellectuals and civil society. Therefore, power is involved both in maintaining and resisting hegemony.

### Synthesis

Power and hegemony are closely linked because **hegemony makes power appear legitimate, natural and acceptable**. Power sustained only by coercion is fragile, while hegemonic power becomes deeper because people themselves begin to accept the dominant worldview.

### Relevant Example

The spread of **consumerism through media, advertising and popular culture** is a good example. Economic power alone cannot explain its influence; repeated cultural messages can make consumption-oriented values appear normal and desirable, showing how power operates through hegemony.

### Conclusion

**Power provides the capacity to dominate, while hegemony converts domination into consent.**  
Therefore, Gramsci shows that durable power depends not only on force but also on control over **ideas, culture and common sense**.

## Q11. Legitimacy adds positive value to Political Authority and Obligation — 2024 (20 Marks)

### What is Legitimacy?

**Legitimacy** means the acceptance of a ruler, law or political institution as having a **rightful claim to rule**. It makes people obey authority not merely because they fear punishment, but because they consider its exercise of power valid and acceptable. Thus, legitimacy gives moral and political acceptance to power.

For **Max Weber**, political authority is essentially **legitimate power**. He identifies traditional, charismatic and rational-legal grounds of legitimacy. Therefore, legitimacy distinguishes mere possession of power from an accepted **right to command**.

### How Legitimacy adds Positive Value to Political Authority and Obligation

**1. Converts Power into Authority:**  
Power only means the capacity to make others obey, even through force. Legitimacy gives that power an accepted **right to rule**, converting naked power into political authority. Hence, people recognise commands as rightful rather than merely unavoidable.

**2. Creates Political Obligation:**  
Legitimacy creates a sense among citizens that they **ought to obey** laws and institutions. **Rousseau** suggests that law becomes acceptable when it reflects the consent of those governed. Thus, obedience moves from fear of punishment towards a sense of political duty.

**3. Reduces Dependence on Coercion:**  
A legitimate authority does not need to use force continuously because citizens largely comply voluntarily. This makes governance more stable and reduces conflict between rulers and ruled. Therefore, legitimacy strengthens authority by creating **willing obedience and consent**.

**4. Strengthens Democratic Authority:**  
In modern democracies, legitimacy is maintained through **rights, free and fair elections, an independent judiciary and freedom of the press**. These mechanisms allow people to accept political authority because they have a role in determining and controlling it.

**5. Makes Authority Accountable:**  
Legitimacy is not given permanently; rulers must continuously satisfy the purposes for which political institutions exist. **Habermas** shows that when institutions fail to meet social expectations, confidence declines and a **legitimation crisis** develops. Thus, legitimacy also places limits on political authority.

### Criticism

**Gramsci** questions whether all legitimacy is based on genuine consent. Dominant groups can **manufacture consent** through culture, education and civil society, making their interests appear as common sense. Therefore, an authority may appear legitimate even when unequal power relations remain hidden.

**Foucault** similarly shows that acceptance may be shaped through **discourses, knowledge and governed mentality**. People may obey because social institutions have already shaped what they consider normal and acceptable. Hence, legitimacy does not automatically prove that an authority is morally just.

### Synthesis

Legitimacy nevertheless remains essential because political rule based entirely on coercion cannot easily create stable political obligation. However, legitimacy should be based on **genuine consent, rights, participation and accountability**, rather than mere passive acceptance.

Thus, legitimate authority creates a reciprocal relationship: citizens acquire an obligation to obey rightful laws, while rulers acquire an obligation to **justify their power and remain responsive to citizens**. Legitimacy therefore gives positive value to both authority and obligation.

### Relevant Example

In a **constitutional democracy**, citizens normally obey laws such as taxation not simply because the state can punish non-compliance. They also recognise that such laws are made through constitutionally authorised institutions and can be challenged through legal procedures. Here, legitimacy converts state power into authority and creates political obligation.

### Conclusion

Legitimacy transforms **power into rightful authority and obedience into political obligation**.  
Its positive value, however, remains strongest when legitimacy rests on **genuine consent, participation, rights and accountability**.

## Q12. Explain Macpherson’s View on Power — 2025 (10 Marks)

### What is Power?

**Power** means the capacity to influence the actions and opportunities of others. **C.B. Macpherson**, however, does not see all power as the same; he asks whether power is used to **dominate others or to develop human capacities**. This leads to his distinction between extractive and developmental power.

### Macpherson’s Ideas on Power

**1. Extractive Power:**  
**Extractive power** is the capacity of one person or class to gain advantages by controlling or exploiting others. Macpherson argues that unequal capitalist societies allow those controlling resources to possess much greater power than workers. Thus, inequality converts economic advantage into domination.

**2. Developmental Power:**  
**Developmental power** means the capacity of individuals to develop and exercise their human abilities and creative freedom. Power, therefore, need not simply mean control over another person. It can also mean giving people the real opportunity to **grow, participate and realise their capacities**.

**3. Goal of an Egalitarian Society:**  
Macpherson argues that a democratic society should **minimise or eliminate extractive power and maximise developmental power**. Everyone should have broadly equal opportunities for development rather than allowing a small group to possess both economic and political power. This connects power with substantive democracy.

### Synthesis

Macpherson therefore changes the question from simply **“Who has power?” to “What kind of power should society promote?”** A democratic society should reduce power exercised **over others** and expand the power of every person **to develop themselves**.

### Relevant Example

Consider a workplace where a few owners alone control decisions while workers have little say; this reflects **extractive power**. A workplace that gives workers meaningful participation, training and opportunities to develop their abilities moves closer to Macpherson’s idea of **developmental power**.

### Conclusion

For Macpherson, a just democracy must move from **extractive domination towards developmental empowerment**.  
Thus, genuine equality requires not merely equal voting rights, but **equal opportunities to exercise and develop human capacities**.

## Q13. Legitimacy is always a ‘Social Construct’ — 2026 (10 Marks)

### What is Legitimacy?

**Legitimacy** means the acceptance of a ruler, law or political system as having a **rightful claim to rule**. It converts mere power into authority because people obey not only due to force but because they believe the rule is valid and acceptable.

### Why can Legitimacy be called a Social Construct?

**1. It depends on social beliefs:**  
What people consider legitimate changes across societies and historical periods. **Weber’s** traditional, charismatic and legal-rational authority itself shows that different societies construct different grounds for accepting political rule. Thus, legitimacy depends greatly on shared social beliefs and values.

**2. Consent itself can be socially produced:**  
**Gramsci** argues that legitimacy can emerge through **manufactured consent**, while **Foucault** shows how discourses and social norms shape what people consider normal and acceptable. Therefore, legitimacy is not simply given; institutions and social practices continuously help construct it.

### Why Legitimacy is not *only* a Social Construct?

**1. Acceptance alone is not enough:**  
A government may enjoy social acceptance, but that does not automatically make every exercise of authority legitimate. Modern legitimacy also requires standards such as **rule of law, rights, impartial institutions and constitutional limits**. Therefore, legitimacy cannot be reduced merely to what society happens to believe.

**2. Legitimacy also requires justification:**  
**Habermas** links legitimacy with democratic participation and rational public deliberation. Authority must be capable of being **publicly justified to citizens**, rather than simply gaining passive acceptance. Hence, legitimate rule involves both social recognition and normative principles such as participation and fairness.

### Conclusion

Legitimacy is substantially a **social construct because the right to rule depends on shared beliefs, values and consent**.  
However, durable democratic legitimacy also requires **rights, rule of law and public justification**, not social acceptance alone.
