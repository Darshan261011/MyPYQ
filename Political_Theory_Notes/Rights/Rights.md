## Q1. Examine the multi-cultural perspectives on rights. (2012) (10 Marks)

### What is Multiculturalism?

**Multiculturalism** deals with how a state should respond to **cultural diversity** based on religion, ethnicity and nationality. It argues that treating everyone exactly the same may ignore the special problems faced by minority cultures. Hence, rights should also recognise **cultural differences and identities**.

### Key ideas of the multicultural perspective on rights

**1. Equal rights alone may not be enough:**  
**Will Kymlicka** argues that universal citizenship can lead to the domination of the majority culture. Therefore, minorities may require **group-differentiated rights** such as cultural and educational rights, representation and self-government. This helps them participate as equals without losing their identity.

**2. Rights should also recognise identity:**  
The multicultural approach says individuals are connected to the culture in which they live. **Charles Taylor's politics of recognition** argues that ignoring or misrecognising cultural identity can marginalise minorities. Thus, equality requires not only identical rights but also **recognition of difference**.

**3. Rights cannot come from one culture alone:**  
**Bhikhu Parekh** argues that principles of rights should not be based only on the values of one dominant culture. They should emerge through **dialogue among different cultures**. However, cultural practices can be protected only so long as they do not cause serious harm to individuals or society.

### Criticism

A major problem is that protecting groups may sometimes harm **individuals within those groups**. **Susan Moller Okin** argues that cultural or religious group rights may protect practices that discriminate against women. Similarly, excessive group rights may strengthen divisions rather than common citizenship.

### Synthesis + contemporary example

Therefore, multiculturalism should combine **recognition of cultural difference with minimum universal individual rights**. India reflects such a balance through **Articles 29–30 protecting minority cultural and educational rights and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules protecting tribal communities**, while all remain within the constitutional framework of fundamental rights.

### Conclusion

Multiculturalism makes rights more meaningful by recognising that people live within different cultural identities.  
However, **cultural rights must complement, not replace, basic universal individual rights.**

## Q2. Comment on Cultural Relativism. (2022) (10 Marks)

### What is Cultural Relativism?

**Cultural relativism** means that beliefs, values and rights should be understood in the context of the **culture in which people live**. It questions the idea that one universal standard can judge every society. Thus, what is considered right or wrong may differ across cultures.

### Key ideas of Cultural Relativism

**1. Culture shapes our understanding:**  
Cultural relativists argue that individuals are deeply influenced by their social and cultural environment. Therefore, ideas of morality, rights and the good life cannot be completely separated from culture. **Franz Boas** developed this approach against judging other cultures through one's own standards.

**2. No culture should become the universal standard:**  
Cultural relativism rejects **ethnocentrism**, where one culture considers its own values superior and judges others accordingly. Different cultures may have different but meaningful ways of organising social life. Hence, cultural practices should first be understood in their own context rather than immediately judged from outside.

**3. Challenge to universal human rights:**  
Cultural relativists question whether the same conception of rights can simply be imposed everywhere. It is also argued that **culture is an intrinsic part of personality** and rights cannot be completely independent of culture. Therefore, human rights need some sensitivity to social and cultural diversity.

### Criticism

The danger is that extreme cultural relativism can make it difficult to condemn serious injustice. **James Rachels** argues that if every practice is accepted merely because it belongs to a culture, cultural relativism may lead towards **moral nihilism** and weaken universal human rights. Some basic protections, therefore, must apply irrespective of culture.

### Synthesis + contemporary example

A better approach is to combine **respect for cultural diversity with minimum universal human rights**. For example, debates over religious dress and cultural practices in multicultural societies show that states should respect cultural identity, but such recognition must remain compatible with basic dignity, equality and individual freedom. This balance reflects the continuing tension between cultural diversity and universal rights.

### Conclusion

Cultural relativism reminds us that **rights cannot be understood completely outside their cultural context**.  
However, cultural diversity should be respected **without sacrificing a minimum universal standard of human dignity and rights**.

## Q3. Comment on: Idea of Natural Rights (2015) (10 Marks)

### What are Natural Rights?

**Natural rights** are rights which a person possesses **by virtue of being human**, even before the creation of the state.  
They do not depend upon the government for their existence or recognition.  
The state is created mainly to **recognise and protect these pre-existing rights**.

### Key ideas of Natural Rights

**1. Rights exist prior to the State:**  
Natural-rights theory holds that individuals possessed certain rights even in the **state of nature**.  
Therefore, these rights are not gifts given by rulers or governments.  
This makes the **individual an end and the state a means** for protecting rights.

**2. Life, Liberty and Property:**  
**John Locke** gave the most influential formulation of natural rights as **life, liberty and property**.  
Individuals create government through the social contract mainly to protect these rights.  
Since these rights are **inalienable**, a government violating them loses its moral justification.

**3. Rights are based on human reason:**  
Natural rights are considered **self-evident and based on reason**, rather than merely on laws made by the state.  
Locke believed reason teaches individuals not to harm others in their life, liberty or possessions.  
Thus, natural rights place limits upon political authority and protect individual freedom.

### Criticism

**Bentham and Hobbes** argue that rights become meaningful only when they are recognised and enforced by the **state and law**. Bentham therefore criticised natural rights as vague and abstract, while **Edmund Burke** also argued that rights grow out of society and culture rather than an abstract state of nature. Thus, natural-rights theory may underestimate the importance of legal and social institutions.

### Synthesis + Example

Natural rights provide the **moral foundation**, while law gives those rights practical protection.  
For example, the modern **human-rights framework** carries forward the natural-rights idea that every person possesses basic rights, but these are also protected through constitutions, laws and international institutions.

### Conclusion

Natural rights transformed politics by making the **individual and his basic freedoms prior to the authority of the state**.  
Though rights need legal protection, the natural-rights idea remains the philosophical foundation of modern human rights.

## Q4. Analyse the relationship between natural rights and human rights.(2013) (15 Marks)

### Rights + background of Natural Rights and Human Rights

**Rights**, according to **Laski**, are those conditions of life without which a person cannot be at his best. **Natural rights** developed from social-contract theory and hold that some rights belong to man even before the state. **Locke** identified **life, liberty and property** as such inherent rights.

**Human rights** are the modern and wider form of this idea, belonging to every person simply because he is human. After the experience of the Second World War, they received global recognition through the **UDHR, 1948**. Thus, an old philosophical claim of natural rights gradually became a modern framework of universal rights.

### Main relationship between Natural Rights and Human Rights

**1. Philosophical foundation:**  
Natural rights provide the **philosophical foundation** of human rights. Both begin from the idea that certain rights belong to individuals because of their human nature, rather than because a government chooses to grant them. Human rights therefore carry forward the natural-rights concern for human dignity and freedom.

**2. From abstract idea to recognised rights:**  
Natural rights were mainly **moral and philosophical claims**, existing independently of state recognition. Human rights convert these claims into more clearly recognised standards through constitutions and international institutions. Thus, human rights give a more practical and institutional form to the natural-rights tradition.

**3. Human rights have a wider scope:**  
Natural-rights thinkers like Locke mainly stressed **life, liberty and property** and protection of the individual against the state. Human rights expanded this idea to include **civil, political, social, economic and cultural rights**. Therefore, human rights are broader than the classical conception of natural rights.

**4. Same universality, stronger protection:**  
Both natural rights and human rights claim that basic rights belong to human beings universally. However, human rights add mechanisms of **legal recognition, international standards and state responsibility**. As **Jack Donnelly** notes, modern human rights continue to be grounded in the inherent dignity and equality of human beings.

### Criticism

The relationship is not completely smooth because **natural rights are abstract moral claims**, whereas human rights require legal institutions for their actual enjoyment. Further, cultural relativists question whether one universal conception of rights can apply equally to every culture. Thus, universality must also deal with differences of culture, society and political context.

### Synthesis

Natural rights can therefore be seen as the **moral source**, while human rights are their **modern, secular, broader and institutionalised expression**. Human rights preserve the natural-rights idea of inherent dignity but make it more practical by extending both the range and protection of rights.

### Relevant example

The **UDHR, 1948** reflects this evolution clearly: it accepts the natural-rights idea that all human beings possess inherent dignity and rights, but expresses these rights through an international framework. Later human-rights instruments further expanded protection beyond basic liberty to social, economic and cultural rights.

### Conclusion

Natural rights supplied the **philosophical language of inherent and inalienable rights**, while human rights transformed it into a broader modern framework.  
Hence, the relationship is essentially one of **continuity, expansion and institutionalisation**.

## Q5. What do you understand by three generations of Human Rights? (2018) (15 Marks)

### Rights and background of Human Rights

**Rights**, according to **Harold Laski**, are those conditions of life without which a person cannot be at his best. Human rights go further and claim that certain basic rights belong to every person simply **by virtue of being human**. Thus, they aim to protect human freedom, equality and dignity.

Modern human rights developed especially after the experience of the **Second World War**, leading to the **UDHR in 1948**. Over time, rights expanded from individual liberties to socio-economic and finally collective concerns. **Karel Vasak** explained this evolution through the idea of **three generations of human rights**.

### Three Generations of Human Rights

**1. First Generation – Civil and Political Rights:**  
These are mainly rights of **liberty** and protect individuals from excessive state interference. They include rights such as **life, liberty, political participation and freedom of expression**. Inspired by the natural-rights tradition of **John Locke**, they are largely seen as negative rights requiring restraint on state power.

**2. Second Generation – Socio-economic Rights:**  
These rights emerged from concerns of **social justice and material equality** and require a more active role of the state. They include rights to **education, health, work and adequate living conditions**. Unlike first-generation rights, these are mainly positive rights because their fulfilment needs state resources and intervention.

**3. Third Generation – Collective or Solidarity Rights:**  
These rights belong not merely to isolated individuals but also to **communities and peoples**. They include the right to **development, peace, self-determination and a healthy environment**. Since such problems cross national borders, their fulfilment usually requires cooperation among states and societies.

### Criticism

A major limitation is that dividing rights into separate generations may create an artificial distinction between them. **Martha Nussbaum's capability approach** shows that rights are deeply interconnected—for example, without education and health, a person may not be able to exercise political freedom effectively. Therefore, one generation cannot be realised properly without the others.

Secondly, the framework assumes a broadly **universal understanding of human rights**, which cultural relativists question. Different societies may give different importance to individual, community and cultural claims. Thus, the challenge is to respect cultural diversity without allowing it to weaken minimum standards of human dignity and freedom.

### Synthesis

The three generations should therefore not be seen as **three separate or competing sets of rights**, but as stages in the expansion of human dignity. Civil-political, socio-economic and collective rights are **universal, indivisible and interdependent**, and must strengthen one another.

### Relevant Example

**Climate change** shows the importance of all three generations together. A healthy environment is a third-generation concern, but environmental degradation also affects first-generation rights like life and second-generation rights like health and livelihood. Thus, contemporary problems show the **interdependence of different generations of rights**.

### Conclusion

Karel Vasak's three generations explain how human rights have expanded from **liberty → equality → solidarity**.  
However, their real significance lies not in separation, but in their **combined protection of human dignity and well-being**.

## Q6. Human Rights are complex and contested social practice that organises relations between Individuals, Society and the State. Comment. (2022) (15 Marks)

### Rights + background of Human Rights

**Rights**, according to **Harold Laski**, are those conditions of life without which a person cannot develop himself fully. Human rights are those basic claims which belong to every person simply **because he or she is human**. They protect human dignity, freedom and equality.

Modern human rights developed strongly after the atrocities of the **Second World War** and were given global expression through the **UDHR, 1948**. They now include civil-political, socio-economic and cultural rights. Thus, human rights are not only legal rules but also claims made by individuals and groups against power.

### Why are Human Rights complex and contested?

**1. Universal rights versus cultural diversity:**  
Human rights claim to be universal, but different societies may interpret dignity, freedom and community differently. **Sally Engle Merry** shows that universal human-rights norms are often adapted when applied in local cultural contexts. Therefore, their meaning and application remain continuously debated.

**2. Different rights may create different priorities:**  
Human rights cover **civil, political, economic, social and cultural dimensions**, which may sometimes compete in practice. Societies may disagree over whether liberty, equality, development or cultural claims should receive greater emphasis. Hence, human rights operate through continuous negotiation rather than through one fixed understanding.

### How do Human Rights organise relations between Individuals, Society and the State?

**1. Individual and State:**  
Human rights give individuals claims against arbitrary state power, but at the same time the **state is the main institution expected to protect those rights**. Thus, the state has a dual position: it may be both the **guarantor and the violator** of human rights. This creates the need for law, accountability and mechanisms of redress.

**2. Individual and Society:**  
Human rights also shape how individuals live within society by protecting dignity while recognising collective concerns. Civil society groups and social movements bring violations into public debate and demand changes from the state. Therefore, rights connect individual claims with **collective social action**.

### Criticism

The universal language of human rights itself faces criticism because it may sometimes ignore cultural and social differences. **Cultural relativists** argue that rights cannot be completely separated from the culture in which individuals live, while excessive relativism can also allow injustice to escape criticism. Thus, the central difficulty is balancing **universal dignity with cultural diversity**.

### Synthesis

Human rights should therefore be understood neither merely as abstract universal principles nor simply as products of particular cultures. They are an **evolving social practice**, shaped through interaction among individuals, society, states and international institutions. As **Upendra Baxi** suggests, the language of human rights continues to develop through struggles of marginalised and oppressed groups.

### Relevant Example

The **right to a healthy environment** shows this interaction clearly. An individual requires protection from pollution, society demands sustainable development, while the state must regulate economic activity and protect public welfare. Such newer **third-generation rights** show how human rights expand as new collective problems emerge.

### Conclusion

Human rights are complex because they constantly balance **individual freedom, social interests and state authority**.  
Their contested nature does not reduce their importance; rather, it makes them an evolving language of **dignity, justice and accountability**.

## Q7. “The implementation of human rights is regarded as a matter of changing the conduct of States.” Comment. (2016) (15 Marks)

### Rights + background of Human Rights

**Rights**, according to **Harold Laski**, are those conditions of life without which a person cannot develop himself fully. Human rights are those basic rights available to every person **simply because he or she is human**. They protect dignity, liberty and equality.

Modern human rights gained importance after the atrocities of the **Second World War** and were expressed through the **UDHR, 1948**. Later, the ICCPR and ICESCR expanded these standards into civil-political and socio-economic rights. But their actual enjoyment ultimately depends upon implementation by states. 

### Why is implementation of Human Rights a matter of changing State conduct?

**1. State is the main guarantor of rights:**  
Most human rights have to be protected through **laws, institutions and public policies**, which are largely controlled by the state. Therefore, merely declaring a right internationally does not automatically improve people's lives. The state must change its laws and administrative practices to make those rights effective. 

**2. State can also become the main violator:**  
Human rights often place limits on how governments use **police power, punishment, detention and political authority**. Therefore, implementation also means making states stop practices that violate dignity and liberty. Human-rights norms seek to make governmental power more accountable to individuals.

### How is implementation achieved by changing State conduct?

**1. International norms influence domestic behaviour:**  
Documents such as the **UDHR, ICCPR and ICESCR** set standards which states are expected to incorporate into domestic laws and policies. International monitoring and review create scrutiny and pressure on governments to bring their conduct closer to these standards. 

**2. Accountability creates pressure for reform:**  
International institutions, courts, civil society and human-rights organisations can expose violations and demand corrective action. Public advocacy, legal action and monitoring therefore push governments to **change laws, institutions and administrative practices**. Thus, implementation is largely about converting formal commitments into actual state behaviour. 

### Criticism

However, reducing human-rights implementation only to changing state conduct is too narrow. **State sovereignty, cultural differences and unequal institutional capacity** can limit external pressure, while many violations may also arise from social groups, corporations or non-state actors. Therefore, state reform is necessary, but by itself may not be sufficient. 

### Synthesis

Human rights implementation should therefore combine **international standards with domestic legal reform, accountable institutions and active civil society**. The state remains the central actor because it possesses law-making and coercive power, but lasting protection requires participation from society and international institutions as well. 

### Relevant Example

The **right against arbitrary detention** shows this clearly. International recognition alone cannot protect an individual unless the state changes police procedures, provides judicial safeguards and punishes unlawful detention. Thus, a human right becomes meaningful when **state conduct changes on the ground**.

### Conclusion

Human rights move from **principle to practice mainly through transformation of state behaviour**.  
Yet effective implementation requires not only a responsible state, but also **law, accountability, civil society and international oversight**.

## Q8. Discuss the doctrine of ‘rights as trumps’. (2019) (10 Marks)

### What are Rights?

**Rights** are justified claims which protect the freedom, dignity and development of individuals. They also place limits on what the state and society can do to a person. Thus, rights create a protected sphere for the individual against arbitrary power.

### Key ideas of ‘Rights as Trumps’

**1. Rights over collective welfare:**  
**Ronald Dworkin**, in *Taking Rights Seriously*, argues that individual rights should act as **“trumps” over collective goals**. A government cannot sacrifice a person's basic right simply because doing so may benefit a larger number of people. Thus, rights limit pure utilitarian reasoning.

**2. Protection against majority rule:**  
Rights protect individuals and minorities from the preferences of the majority. For Dworkin, rights are not merely one interest among many, but strong claims against the **state and majority pressure**. This protects individual dignity and equality in a liberal democracy.

**3. Role of constitutional courts:**  
The doctrine gives an important role to **judicial review**, because courts can protect rights when majority decisions threaten them. Thus, constitutional rights act as limits on ordinary political decisions. This helps prevent the **tyranny of the majority**.

### Criticism

A major criticism is that giving rights such strong priority may give excessive power to **unelected judges**. Courts may overturn decisions made through democratic institutions, creating the danger of **judicial overreach**. Thus, the doctrine creates tension between protection of rights and democratic majority rule.

### Synthesis

Dworkin’s doctrine is valuable because democracy cannot mean only **majority rule**; it must also protect basic individual rights. At the same time, judicial protection of rights should operate within constitutional limits so that protection of liberty does not completely replace democratic decision-making.

### Relevant Example

Suppose a majority wants to ban the **peaceful speech of an unpopular minority** merely because most people dislike its views. Under the idea of **rights as trumps**, the individual's basic freedom of expression should receive protection even against majority preference.

### Conclusion

Dworkin’s doctrine makes rights a **strong shield against state power and majority domination**.  
It therefore seeks to balance **democracy with the protection of individual dignity and liberty**.

## Q9. Assess the significance of the right to property in political theory. (2020) (15 Marks)

### What are Rights?

**Rights** are claims and conditions necessary for the freedom and development of individuals. They protect individuals from arbitrary interference and also create obligations on the state. Thus, rights define the relationship between the **individual, society and political authority**.

### Who gave the idea of Right to Property and why?

**John Locke** gave the most influential defence of property as a **natural right**, along with life and liberty. He argued that when a person mixes his **labour with nature**, the product becomes his property. Therefore, property arises from individual labour and exists prior to the state. 

For Locke, protection of property was also an important reason for creating **government through social contract**. Secure property allows individuals to enjoy the fruits of their labour without arbitrary interference. Hence, the state exists partly to protect property, liberty and individual independence. 

### Significance of Right to Property

**1. Foundation of individual liberty:**  
Property gives individuals a degree of **economic independence from the state and others**. Control over one's possessions and labour enables a person to make independent choices. Thus, property became closely connected with liberty in the liberal tradition. 

**2. Economic and social significance:**  
Secure property rights can encourage investment, entrepreneurship and economic activity. **Hernando de Soto** argues that formal property rights allow poorer people to use their assets as collateral and participate more effectively in the economy. Thus, property may support both economic development and social stability. 

### Criticism

**Karl Marx** gives the strongest criticism of private property, especially private ownership of the **means of production**. He argues that under capitalism, property becomes concentrated in the hands of the bourgeoisie, allowing them to profit from workers' labour. Thus, property can create **class inequality and exploitation**, rather than freedom for everyone. 

Even within liberal theory, property cannot be treated as completely absolute. **John Rawls** accepts economic inequalities only when institutions are arranged so that they also benefit the **least advantaged**. Therefore, protection of property has to be balanced with distributive justice and social welfare. 

### Synthesis

The right to property should therefore be neither **abolished completely nor treated as unlimited**. It protects individual freedom and economic initiative, but excessive concentration of property can create inequality. A balanced political order must combine **secure property rights with principles of social justice**.

### Relevant Example

Formal recognition of **land or housing ownership** can allow poorer households to use their property as security for credit and economic activity. This reflects **Hernando de Soto's** argument that secure property rights can convert assets into economic opportunities. 

### Conclusion

The right to property remains significant because it connects **liberty, economic independence and political power**.  
However, its legitimacy ultimately depends on balancing **individual ownership with social justice and the interests of the wider society**.

## Q10. The debate on human rights is caught between the limitations of both universalism and cultural relativism. Comment. (2024) (20)

### Rights + background of Human Rights

**Rights** are claims and conditions necessary for the freedom, dignity and development of individuals. **Human rights** belong to every person simply because they are human, irrespective of nationality, religion, race or other status. Thus, they claim a universal moral basis.

Modern human rights developed strongly after the **Second World War** and received global expression through the **UDHR, 1948**. They seek to establish common standards of dignity and freedom for all human beings. However, their claim to universality has continuously faced challenges from cultural diversity.

### Human Rights caught between Universalism and Cultural Relativism

**1. Universalism – common human dignity:**  
Universalists argue that certain rights belong to all human beings irrespective of culture. **Jack Donnelly** links human rights with the inherent dignity of every individual. Therefore, rights provide a common standard against oppression anywhere in the world.

**2. Limitation of Universalism:**  
The problem is that universalism may assume that the same conception of freedom and rights fits every society. Non-Western societies may have different understandings of community, family and social obligations. Thus, universalism can become a **one-size-fits-all approach** insensitive to cultural context.

**3. Cultural Relativism – importance of context:**  
Cultural relativists argue that ideas of rights develop within particular historical and cultural settings. Therefore, rights should be understood through the values and traditions of each society. **Abdullahi An-Na'im** stresses that human-rights norms become meaningful when they are rooted in local cultural frameworks.

**4. Limitation of Cultural Relativism:**  
However, extreme relativism creates another problem: almost any practice may be defended in the name of culture. It becomes difficult to maintain common standards against serious violations of dignity and freedom. Therefore, cultural difference cannot automatically become a justification for denying basic rights.

**5. The central dilemma:**  
Human rights are therefore caught between two dangers: **universalism may ignore difference, while relativism may weaken universality**. The real challenge is to preserve a minimum common standard of human dignity while allowing culturally different ways of interpreting and implementing rights.

### Criticism

**Makau Mutua** criticises universal human-rights discourse for sometimes carrying assumptions of **Western superiority** and presenting Western experiences as universal standards. His critique shows that universality can lose legitimacy when other civilisations are treated merely as recipients of an already fixed model of rights.

At the same time, **Amartya Sen** challenges strong cultural relativism. He argues that cultures, including Asian societies, are internally diverse and contain traditions of reasoning, tolerance and freedom. Therefore, culture cannot be treated as a fixed excuse for denying individual freedom and basic human dignity.

### Synthesis

A middle path is **universal minimum rights with culturally sensitive application**. **Bhikhu Parekh** argues that principles of human society should emerge through **dialogue among civilisations**, rather than being dictated by one culture. Thus, universality should be built through dialogue, not uniformity.

### Relevant Example

**India** provides a useful example: minority cultures receive protection through **Articles 29–30**, while all citizens remain within a wider framework of fundamental rights. Thus, cultural identity can be accommodated without abandoning a common constitutional commitment to dignity and equality.

### Conclusion

Human rights cannot survive through **rigid universalism or unlimited cultural relativism alone**.  
A workable approach combines **minimum universal human dignity with dialogue, pluralism and cultural sensitivity**.

## Q11. “Rights of the man are separate and independent of the State.” Critically analyze the perspective of Laski. (20) (2026)

### Laski's meaning of Rights

For **Harold Laski**, rights are those **conditions of life without which a person cannot seek to be at his best**. Rights are therefore necessary for the development of human personality and dignity. They are not merely favours granted by the government.

### Relationship between Rights and the State

Laski does not treat the state as the **creator of all rights**; rather, the state should recognise, protect and create conditions for their enjoyment. His pluralism rejects the idea of an unlimited sovereign state and distributes authority among different social associations. Thus, the state exists to serve human development rather than dominate it.

### Why are Rights separate and independent of the State?

**1. Rights arise from human and social needs:**  
For Laski, rights arise because certain conditions are necessary for individuals to develop their personality. Their moral importance therefore does not begin only when the state passes a law. A person can make a legitimate claim against the state itself when those basic conditions are denied.

**2. Rights become a limitation on State power:**  
If the state itself were the sole creator of rights, whatever the state withdrew could no longer be called a right. There would then be no independent standard for judging an unjust or authoritarian government. Laski's pluralism therefore treats unlimited state sovereignty as dangerous to liberty and human rights.

### How are Rights separate and independent of the State?

**1. Society exists beyond the State:**  
Individuals belong to families, communities, associations and other social institutions besides the state. Laski argues that people's loyalties to such associations are **partial and proportional**, rather than belonging absolutely to the state. Therefore, social life cannot be reduced completely to state authority.

**2. Rights can be claimed against the State:**  
Rights provide individuals with a moral basis to oppose arbitrary political authority. The state must justify its actions according to the conditions necessary for human freedom and development. Thus, rights function as **limits upon sovereignty**, rather than simply products of sovereignty.

### Criticism

**1. Legal-positivist criticism:**  
**Hobbes and Bentham** argue that rights cannot effectively exist without law and the state. Bentham regarded abstract natural rights as vague and uncertain because only legally recognised rights have clear institutional protection. Thus, complete independence from the state may make rights difficult to enforce.

**2. T. H. Green's criticism:**  
A right may have moral value outside the state, but its meaningful enjoyment often requires positive state action. **T. H. Green** therefore argues that liberty requires rights and **“rights demand state.”** Education, health and welfare cannot become effective merely through limiting government; they also require government action.

**3. The statement cannot be taken absolutely:**  
Laski himself should not be understood as saying that rights exist in complete isolation from political institutions. Rights may be **socially and morally prior to state authority**, but law, courts and public institutions are required to protect them in practice. Therefore, independence from the state does not mean absence of the state.

### Synthesis

The better reading of Laski is that **the state does not morally own or create human rights, but it remains necessary for their legal realisation**. Rights provide standards for judging state power, while the state provides institutions for protecting those rights. Hence, rights and state are distinct but mutually connected.

### Relevant Example

Consider a person's **freedom of expression**. Its moral importance does not disappear simply because a government wishes to restrict criticism; in this sense the right stands independently against state power. Yet courts and constitutional institutions are necessary to protect that freedom when it is violated.

### Conclusion

Laski rejects the idea that the **State is the ultimate source and master of rights**.  
Rights arise from the needs of human personality and society, while the legitimate state must **recognise, protect and realise them**.
