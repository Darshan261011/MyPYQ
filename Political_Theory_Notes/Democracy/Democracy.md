## Q1. Examine the ‘Participatory Model of Democracy’ (2011) – 30 Marks

### What is Participatory Model of Democracy?

**1. Democracy beyond elections:**  
Participatory democracy means people should not participate only by **voting once in elections**. Citizens should have a continuous role in governance and decision-making. It therefore tries to bring **political power closer to ordinary people**.

**2. Direct participation:**  
People can participate through **initiative, referendum, recall, RTI and social audit**. They may also participate in local meetings and watchdog institutions to ensure accountability. Thus, citizens become **active participants rather than passive voters**.

### Key ideas of Participatory Model of Democracy

**1. Responsible citizenship:**  
**Carole Pateman** argues that participation creates **responsible citizens**. By participating, people understand the complexities of administration and public problems. Participation therefore also acts as a process of **political education and self-development**.

**2. Popular sovereignty:**  
Participatory democracy tries to give real meaning to the idea that **sovereignty belongs to the people**. Instead of leaving decision-making completely to representatives, citizens themselves influence laws and policies. This makes democracy more **inclusive and legitimate**.

**3. Liberty through participation:**  
For **Rousseau**, liberty and political authority can be reconciled when citizens participate in making the laws they obey. Such participation promotes individual development and helps in creating the **general will**. Thus, participation connects individual freedom with the common good.

**4. Continuous political engagement:**  
**Benjamin Barber**, in *Strong Democracy*, argues that democracy should be more than periodic elections. Citizens should continuously participate in discussion, policy-making and public decisions. Such engagement can improve **civic knowledge, political efficacy and accountability**.

### Criticism

**1. Problem of scale:**  
Participatory democracy works more easily in **small communities and local institutions**. In large and diverse modern states, continuous direct participation by millions of citizens may become difficult. Therefore, complete direct democracy may not be practically scalable.

**2. Unequal participation – Robert Dahl:**  
**Robert Dahl** points out that participation itself may not always be equal. Better-informed, educated or more articulate citizens may dominate discussions while weaker sections remain unheard. Thus, participation alone does not automatically guarantee **political equality**.

**3. Efficiency problem:**  
Continuous consultation, meetings and citizen involvement can make decision-making **slow and time-consuming**. Modern governments often deal with highly technical and urgent issues requiring expertise. Excessive participation can therefore create tension between **democratic involvement and administrative efficiency**.

### Synthesis

**1.** Participatory democracy need not completely replace representative democracy. Representative institutions can handle large-scale governance, while **referendums, Gram Sabhas, social audits, RTI and local participation** can deepen democracy between elections.

**2.** Participation also becomes stronger when combined with **deliberation**. As **Habermas** suggests, participation along with meaningful public discussion can improve the legitimacy of public decisions. Thus, democracy requires both **representation and active citizenship**.

### Relevant Example

**Participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, Brazil** allows citizens to directly influence the allocation of parts of the municipal budget. It has been associated with greater transparency, citizen involvement and attention to community priorities, showing how participatory democracy can work effectively at the local level.

### Conclusion

Participatory democracy transforms citizens from **occasional voters into active participants in governance**.  
Its practical limits can be addressed by combining participation with **representative institutions, decentralisation and deliberation**.

## Q2. Issues of debate in contemporary democratic theory (2012) – 10 Marks

### What is democracy and its different forms?

**Democracy** means government based on the participation and consent of the people; **J. S. Mill** calls it *government by discussion*. Democracy can take different forms such as **representative, participatory, deliberative and cosmopolitan democracy**. Each model differs mainly on how people should participate and how democratic decisions should be made.

### Key issues of debate in contemporary democratic theory

**1. Representation vs participation:**  
A major debate is whether democracy should remain mainly **representative**, where people elect rulers, or become more **participatory and deliberative**. **Carole Pateman** stresses greater direct participation, while representation remains necessary for governing large societies.

**2. Inclusion and political equality:**  
Another question is whether every section of society actually gets an **equal voice** in democracy. **Iris Marion Young** stresses inclusion of minorities and marginalized groups, while economic inequality can allow wealth and power to weaken genuine political equality.

**3. Globalization and technology:**  
**David Held** raises the problem of democratic accountability when important decisions increasingly move beyond the nation-state. At the same time, digital technology can increase participation and information, but can also produce **misinformation, echo chambers and weaker public debate**.

### Synthesis

Contemporary democratic theory therefore is not about choosing one single model. **Representation provides practicality, participation provides involvement, and deliberation provides reasoned decision-making**, while inclusion is needed to make these processes genuinely democratic.

### Relevant example

**Participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, Brazil** combines representative government with direct citizen involvement in deciding parts of public expenditure. It shows how representative democracy can be supplemented by participatory practices.

### Conclusion

Contemporary democratic theory seeks to make democracy **more representative, participatory, inclusive and accountable**.  
Its central challenge is to adapt democratic principles to changing **social, economic, global and technological conditions**.

## Q3. Comment on Deliberative Democracy (2019) – 10 Marks

### What is Deliberative Democracy?

**Deliberative democracy** means democratic decisions should come through **discussion, reasoning and debate**, not merely by counting votes. Citizens should listen to different views and give reasons for their positions. **Habermas** connects this with rational discussion in the **public sphere**.

### Key ideas of Deliberative Democracy

**1. Reasoned debate rather than head-counting:**  
Democracy should not simply aggregate already existing preferences through majority voting. People should discuss, question and examine different arguments before deciding. Thus, the **quality of the decision-making process** becomes important.

**2. Equality, pluralism and free discussion:**  
All affected groups should get an opportunity to express their views in a **free and non-coercive environment**. Different values and opinions should be respected rather than silenced by the majority. In **Joshua Cohen’s** formulation, the important force should be the **“force of argument.”**

**3. Deliberation as a source of legitimacy:**  
For **Habermas**, laws gain greater legitimacy when people can participate in rational communication and justify public decisions. Deliberation can also change people's preferences after they hear different viewpoints. Therefore, democracy becomes more than simply choosing rulers through elections.

### Synthesis

Deliberative democracy should not completely replace **elections or representative institutions**. Elections provide the final mechanism of decision, while deliberation makes those decisions **better informed, inclusive and publicly justified**. Thus, voting and deliberation can complement each other.

### Relevant Example

**Citizens’ assemblies** are a good example. A diverse group of citizens studies a public issue, listens to different arguments and then makes recommendations after discussion. This brings the principle of **reasoned public deliberation** into practical democracy.

### Conclusion

Deliberative democracy shifts democracy from merely **counting votes to exchanging reasons**.  
It seeks to make democratic decisions more **inclusive, informed and legitimate**.

## Q4. Explicate the features of Deliberative Democracy (2014) – 15 Marks

### What is Deliberative Democracy?

**1. Democracy through discussion:**  
Deliberative democracy holds that democratic decisions should emerge from **discussion, reasoning and exchange of arguments** among citizens. It focuses not merely on the final decision but also on the **quality of the process** through which it is reached.

**2. Beyond mere voting:**  
It rejects the idea that democracy is simply a **head-counting or majority-voting mechanism**. A decision acquires greater legitimacy when free and equal citizens can discuss its reasons before deciding. Thus, **authentic deliberation** becomes central to democracy.

### Key ideas / features of Deliberative Democracy

**1. Reasoned debate and legitimacy:**  
For **Habermas**, citizens should freely exchange and critically examine reasons in the **public sphere**. Decisions should emerge from rational communication rather than simply from power or majority strength. Thus, deliberation becomes an important source of **democratic legitimacy**.

**2. Inclusion, equality and pluralism:**  
All those affected should get an opportunity to express their views on an **equal footing**. **Gutmann and Thompson** stress inclusion, while **Joshua Cohen** emphasizes a non-coercive environment and respect for pluralism. Different viewpoints therefore become part of democratic decision-making.

**3. Consensus, reflection and accountability:**  
Deliberation tries to find **common ground** rather than immediately imposing the majority view. Discussion can also make citizens reconsider and transform their preferences after hearing others. Further, public officials must justify decisions openly, strengthening **transparency and accountability**.

### Criticism

**1. Problem of unequal voices:**  
**Nancy Fraser** questions whether one common public sphere can really provide equal voice to everyone. Socially stronger groups may dominate discussion while weaker groups remain unheard; hence she stresses the importance of **“counter-publics”** for marginalized sections.

**2. Idealism and practical difficulty:**  
Deliberative democracy assumes that citizens can engage in rational, inclusive and respectful discussion. In reality, deep conflicts, unequal resources and the large size of modern societies make such ideal deliberation difficult to achieve everywhere.

### Synthesis

Deliberative democracy need not replace **elections or representative democracy**. Elections provide a practical mechanism for taking decisions, while deliberation ensures that those decisions are preceded by **participation, reasoning and public justification**.

### Relevant Example

**Citizens’ assemblies** show deliberative democracy in practice, where diverse citizens come together to discuss public issues and consider different arguments before giving recommendations. They demonstrate how **public reasoning can supplement representative institutions**.

### Conclusion

Deliberative democracy moves democracy from merely **counting votes towards exchanging reasons**.  
Its significance lies in making democratic decisions more **inclusive, reasoned, accountable and legitimate**.

## Q5. Deliberative democracy does not have its salience without participation and participatory democracy does not have its credence without deliberations. Comment. (2017) – 15 Marks

### What is Deliberative Democracy?

**1.** Deliberative democracy means democratic decisions should come through **reasoned discussion, debate and exchange of views**. It focuses on the quality of decision-making, not merely on voting. **Habermas** connects it with rational discussion in the public sphere.

**2.** It assumes that citizens should listen to different viewpoints and try to reach an informed collective decision. Thus, democracy becomes more than simple aggregation of preferences. The legitimacy of a decision depends partly on the **quality and inclusiveness of deliberation**.

### How deliberative democracy does not have salience without participation

**1. Participation gives breadth to deliberation:**  
Deliberation becomes democratic only when a wide range of citizens actually participate in it. Without participation, discussion may remain limited to experts or elites. Then it cannot truly reflect the diversity and pluralism of society.

**2. Participation gives legitimacy:**  
Public discussion gains relevance when those affected by decisions have an opportunity to take part. Broad participation brings different experiences and interests into the debate. Otherwise, deliberation may become intellectually sound but **democratically weak**.

### How participatory democracy does not have credence without deliberation

**1. Deliberation gives quality to participation:**  
Participation alone may become only the expression of individual preferences or demands. Through deliberation, citizens examine arguments, question assumptions and consider the common good. Thus, participation becomes **informed rather than merely numerical**.

**2. Deliberation can transform preferences:**  
Citizens may enter the process with fixed interests, but discussion exposes them to other viewpoints. This can make them reconsider their positions and search for common ground. Hence, deliberation gives **depth and credibility** to participation.

### Criticism

**Nancy Fraser** points out that public discussion may not always be genuinely equal because socially powerful groups can dominate the public sphere. She therefore stresses **“counter-publics”** where weaker and marginalized groups can develop and express their voices. This shows that participation and deliberation require real social equality to work properly.

### Synthesis

Participatory and deliberative democracy should therefore be seen as **complementary rather than competing models**. Participation provides the **breadth of voices**, while deliberation provides the **depth of reasoning**; together they improve democratic legitimacy and effectiveness.

### Relevant Example

**Participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, Brazil** combines both ideas. Citizens directly participate in deciding public spending, but the process also requires discussion and consideration of competing priorities before decisions are made.

### Conclusion

**Participation without deliberation may become mere preference-counting, while deliberation without participation may become elitist.**  
A stronger democracy therefore requires both **wide citizen participation and meaningful public reasoning**.

## Q6. Difference between Participatory and Deliberative Democracy (2015) – 10 Marks

### Participatory and Deliberative Democracy

**Participatory democracy** focuses on the **direct involvement of citizens** in decisions affecting their lives. Citizens participate through meetings, referendums, social audits, participatory budgeting and other mechanisms. **Carole Pateman** argues that such participation develops responsible and public-minded citizens.

**Deliberative democracy** focuses on the **quality of discussion before a decision is taken**. Citizens should exchange reasons, listen to different viewpoints and arrive at an informed decision. **Habermas** therefore stresses rational communication and discussion in the public sphere.

### Different dimensions of difference

**1. Primary focus:**  
Participatory democracy asks **“How many people actually participate in decision-making?”** Deliberative democracy asks **“How are decisions discussed and justified?”** Thus, the former stresses the **breadth of participation**, while the latter stresses the **quality of discussion**.

**2. Role of citizens:**  
In participatory democracy, citizens directly take part in **making and implementing decisions**. In deliberative democracy, citizens mainly discuss competing arguments and examine reasons before decisions are made. Thus, one stresses **active involvement**, while the other stresses **reasoned involvement**.

**3. Source of legitimacy:**  
Participatory democracy derives legitimacy from the **direct involvement of the people**. Deliberative democracy derives legitimacy from **free, rational and authentic deliberation**. In Joshua Cohen’s formulation, decisions should emerge through the **force of argument rather than coercion**.

**4. Democratic objective:**  
Participatory democracy seeks to **empower citizens and deepen popular sovereignty**. Deliberative democracy seeks to produce **better-informed and publicly justified decisions**. Hence, participation mainly deepens democratic control, while deliberation improves the reasoning behind that control.

**5. Practical mechanisms:**  
Participatory democracy is expressed through **participatory budgeting, town meetings and community decision-making**. Deliberative democracy commonly works through **citizens’ assemblies, deliberative polls and consensus conferences**. Their mechanisms therefore reflect their different primary purposes.

### Criticism

**Participatory democracy:** **Robert Dahl** points out that intense participation may be difficult in large societies and discussions may be dominated by more articulate or better-informed citizens. It can also make decision-making slow and time-consuming. Thus, greater participation does not automatically produce equality.

**Deliberative democracy:** It can become too **idealistic**, because equal and rational discussion is difficult where social inequalities and deep conflicts exist. **Nancy Fraser** therefore stresses the need for counter-publics so that weaker groups can also develop and express their voices. The challenge is making deliberation genuinely inclusive.

### Synthesis

The two models are **complementary rather than contradictory**. Participation gives democracy the **breadth of citizen involvement**, while deliberation gives it the **depth of informed reasoning**; together they make democracy more inclusive and thoughtful.

### Relevant Example

**Participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, Brazil** shows this combination. Citizens participate directly in decisions about public expenditure, while discussion of different community priorities introduces a deliberative element into participation.

### Conclusion

Participatory democracy emphasizes **who participates**, while deliberative democracy emphasizes **how decisions are discussed and justified**.  
A deeper democracy requires both **meaningful participation and reasoned deliberation**.

## Q7. Comment on Substantive Democracy (2018) – 10 Marks

### What is Substantive Democracy?

**Substantive democracy** goes beyond procedures such as **elections and voting** and asks what democracy actually delivers to citizens. It focuses on **rights, equality, social justice and economic well-being**. Thus, democracy is judged not only by how rulers are chosen, but also by its impact on people's lives.

### Key ideas of Substantive Democracy

**1. Rights and social justice:**  
Free and fair elections alone do not make democracy meaningful if people lack basic rights and opportunities. Substantive democracy therefore seeks protection of **human rights, equality and fair distribution of resources**. Political equality should be supported by social and economic justice.

**2. Justice and fairness – Rawls:**  
**John Rawls** stresses that social institutions should be organised according to principles of fairness. Opportunities and resources should not simply favour those who are already advantaged. Thus, democracy should create conditions in which citizens can enjoy **fair opportunities**, not merely equal voting rights.

**3. Freedom and capabilities – Amartya Sen:**  
**Amartya Sen** argues that democracy should also be judged by whether it expands people's **freedoms and capabilities**. Education, health and social opportunities enable people to actually use their formal rights. Therefore, substantive democracy connects political freedom with the **real capacity to live a meaningful life**.

### Criticism

Substantive democracy creates difficulty in deciding **what outcomes democracy must guarantee**. Different societies may disagree about the proper level of equality, welfare or redistribution, and excessive state intervention may also create concerns about government overreach. Therefore, substantive goals must remain compatible with democratic procedures and individual freedom.

### Synthesis

**Procedural and substantive democracy should complement each other.** Elections give governments democratic authority, while substantive democracy ensures that democracy produces rights, opportunities and human development. **Macpherson** similarly criticises democracy reduced merely to elections and stresses **developmental power and creative freedom**.

### Relevant Example

A democracy that provides **free and fair elections together with public education, healthcare and social-welfare measures** reflects substantive democracy. Such measures try to convert formal political equality into a better quality of life and real opportunities for citizens.

### Conclusion

Substantive democracy asks not only **“Can people vote?” but also “What does democracy enable people to achieve?”**  
Democracy becomes deeper when **political rights are supported by social justice, equality and human development**.

## Q8. Critically examine Macpherson’s views on Democracy (2018) – 15 Marks

### What is democracy and its different kinds?

**Democracy** means political power is ultimately based on the people; **J. S. Mill** describes it as government by discussion. It can take forms such as **representative, participatory, deliberative and substantive democracy**. Macpherson especially examines how democracy should move beyond elections towards **equality and human development**.

### Key ideas in Macpherson’s views on Democracy

**1. Critique of possessive individualism:**  
Macpherson argues that liberal democracy is influenced by **possessive individualism**, where individuals are viewed mainly as owners of themselves and their property. Though liberal democracy promises equality and rights, capitalism can create large economic inequalities. Therefore, formal political equality may exist alongside unequal social and economic power.

**2. Extractive and developmental power:**  
Macpherson distinguishes between **extractive power**, where one person or class exercises power over others, and **developmental power**, which enables individuals to develop their capacities. His ideal is an egalitarian society with minimum extractive power and maximum opportunities for **creative freedom and human development**. This gives democracy a substantive purpose beyond merely choosing rulers.

**3. Beyond procedural democracy:**  
Macpherson criticises contemporary theories represented by **Schumpeter and Dahl** for reducing democracy largely to elections and group competition. He instead favours **participatory democracy**, where citizens actively take part in decisions affecting their lives. Democracy should therefore promote substantive freedom, participation and social equality, not merely voting and representation.

### Criticism

**1. Individual liberty versus social welfare:**  
Macpherson’s emphasis on equality and social welfare can create a tension with individual freedom. Critics argue that increasing collective control to reduce economic inequality may sometimes restrict private choice and autonomy. Therefore, democracy must balance both **social equality and individual liberty**.

**2. Practical problem of participation:**  
His participatory model is easier to imagine at the local level than in very large and diverse societies. Continuous participation by millions of citizens can become difficult, time-consuming and administratively complex. Representative institutions therefore continue to have an important practical role.

**3. Procedural perspective:**  
From the procedural perspective associated with **Schumpeter and Dahl**, Macpherson may expect democracy to perform too many social and developmental functions. Elections, competition and organised groups provide workable mechanisms for modern mass democracy. Thus, substantive democracy may deepen democracy, but cannot entirely replace effective democratic procedures.

### Synthesis

Macpherson’s importance lies in showing that **elections are necessary but not sufficient for democracy**. A workable democracy can combine representative institutions with participation, while also reducing inequality and expanding citizens’ developmental opportunities.

### Relevant Example

**Gram Sabhas in India** reflect part of Macpherson’s participatory ideal. Citizens can directly discuss local development priorities instead of remaining only periodic voters. This brings decision-making closer to people and gives democracy a more developmental and participatory character.

### Conclusion

Macpherson shifts democracy from merely **choosing rulers to developing citizens and reducing unequal power**.  
His contribution is to connect **political participation, equality and human development** with the meaning of democracy.

## Q9. Explicate the features of Representative Democracy (2016) – 15 Marks

### What is democracy and its different kinds?

**Democracy** is a system in which political authority ultimately comes from the people and government remains accountable to them. It may take forms such as **direct, representative, participatory, deliberative and substantive democracy**. These models differ mainly in how citizens participate and how democratic decisions are taken.

### What is Representative Democracy?

**Representative democracy**, or indirect democracy, is a system where citizens **elect representatives to govern and make decisions on their behalf**. It makes democracy workable in large and complex societies where everyone cannot participate directly in every decision. **J.S. Mill and Edmund Burke** are important supporters of representative government.

### Features of Representative Democracy

**1. Free elections and accountability:**  
Citizens exercise their political power mainly by **electing representatives at regular intervals**. **Robert Dahl** stresses regular, free and fair elections as essential to representative democracy. Elections also allow citizens to remove representatives who no longer enjoy their confidence.

**2. Rule of law:**  
Representatives cannot exercise power according to their personal wishes but must act within **law and constitutional limits**. Rule of law protects citizens from arbitrary government and ensures legal equality. Thus, elected power remains limited and accountable.

**3. Separation of powers:**  
Representative democracy distributes authority among different institutions rather than concentrating it in one centre. **Montesquieu** stresses separation among legislature, executive and judiciary to create checks and balances. This reduces the possibility of arbitrary or absolute power.

**4. Political pluralism:**  
Representative democracy allows different **political parties, groups and opinions** to compete within the political system. Citizens can choose between different programmes and representatives. Such pluralism enables diverse social interests to enter the policy-making process.

**5. Majority rule with minority rights:**  
Representative democracy generally allows the majority to form government, but majority power cannot be unlimited. Constitutional rights and civil liberties protect individuals and minorities from the **tyranny of the majority**. Thus, representation must combine popular rule with protection of diversity.

### Criticism

**1. Rousseau – representatives may replace the people:**  
**Rousseau** argues that once legislative power is handed over to representatives, they may pursue their own interests rather than the common interest. Representation can therefore create distance between the people and actual decision-making. He preferred greater direct control of citizens over law-making.

**2. Gandhi – party politics can weaken real participation:**  
**Gandhi** criticised party-based politics and preferred **partyless democracy and village republics**. Representative systems may turn citizens into voters only during elections while political parties dominate public life. His approach therefore seeks democracy closer to the people at the grassroots.

**3. Macpherson – elections are not enough:**  
**Macpherson** criticises contemporary democracy when it becomes merely **procedural**, centred on elections and competition. Formal representation may coexist with social and economic inequalities that limit citizens' real power. Democracy therefore needs greater participation, equality and developmental opportunities in addition to representation.

### Synthesis

Representative democracy remains practical for **large modern societies**, but representation alone cannot exhaust the meaning of democracy. It can be strengthened by **participatory mechanisms, deliberation and substantive equality**, so that citizens influence politics both during and between elections.

### Relevant Example

**India's Lok Sabha** illustrates representative democracy: citizens elect MPs who make laws and hold the government accountable on their behalf. At the same time, constitutional rights, political competition and institutional checks place limits on representative power.

### Conclusion

Representative democracy combines **popular consent with the practicality of elected government**.  
Its strength increases when representation is supported by **accountability, pluralism, participation and protection of rights**.

## Q10. “The goal of good governance will be achieved only by strengthening the grass root level democracy.” Comment. (2016) – 20 Marks

### What is democracy and its different kinds?

**Democracy** means that political authority ultimately rests with the people and government remains accountable to them. It may take forms such as **representative, participatory, deliberative, substantive and grassroots democracy**. Grassroots democracy takes democracy closest to citizens by shifting decision-making towards local institutions and communities.

### How strengthening grassroots democracy promotes good governance

**1. Governance closer to the people:**  
Good governance requires administration to be **responsive to people's actual needs**. Panchayats and municipalities bring decision-making closer to local communities and make representatives directly answerable to them. Thus, decentralisation improves responsiveness and accountability.

**2. Participation and inclusion:**  
Grassroots democracy allows citizens to participate in **planning, implementation and monitoring** rather than remaining passive voters. This can make policies more inclusive and better suited to local priorities. **Kuldeep Mathur** stresses that local institutions need power, resources, training and transparency to perform this role effectively.

**3. Transparency and accountability:**  
Mechanisms such as **Gram Sabhas, social audits and public hearings** allow citizens to examine expenditure and question local authorities. They also provide weaker sections with opportunities to raise grievances directly. Hence, citizen participation can reduce the distance between administration and the governed.

**4. Local knowledge and better development:**  
Local communities often understand their resources and problems better than distant authorities. **Elinor Ostrom** showed how communities can collectively manage common resources, while **Robert Putnam** linked civic engagement and strong local institutions with better governance. Thus, grassroots participation can strengthen both efficiency and social responsibility.

### Criticism

**1. Paul Brass – inadequate devolution:**  
**Paul Brass** points to the reluctance of higher-level political actors to transfer real power to local institutions. Panchayats may therefore exist democratically but lack adequate **functions, funds and functionaries**. Without genuine devolution, grassroots democracy can become more symbolic than effective.

**2. Rajni Kothari – local elite domination:**  
**Rajni Kothari** warns that village institutions can sometimes be controlled by established local elites. In such situations, decentralisation may simply shift power from higher elites to **local elites**, rather than empowering ordinary citizens. Therefore, local democracy does not automatically guarantee inclusive governance.

**3. Institutional and capacity weaknesses:**  
Local bodies may suffer from inadequate resources, weak Gram Sabhas, poor awareness, bureaucratic dependence and corruption. **Niraja Gopal Jayal** therefore stresses stronger capacity-building, transparency and accountability mechanisms. Grassroots institutions need real administrative and financial strength, not merely elected representatives.

### Synthesis

Grassroots democracy is therefore **necessary but not sufficient by itself** for good governance. Local participation must be combined with adequate finances, administrative capacity, rule of law and effective state-level institutions; only then can decentralisation produce accountable and responsive governance.

### Relevant Example

**Hiware Bazar in Maharashtra** shows how community participation and local resource management can improve village development. Local involvement in planning and management demonstrates how grassroots democracy can convert citizen participation into practical developmental outcomes.

### Conclusion

Good governance becomes meaningful when democracy reaches citizens **where they actually live and experience government**.  
Strengthening grassroots institutions with **real power, resources, participation and accountability** therefore provides a strong foundation for good governance.

## Q11. Elitist theory of democracy denies the possibility of democracy as ‘rule of the people’. Elucidate. (2022) – 15 Marks

### What is democracy and its different kinds?

**Democracy** means political authority ultimately belongs to the people and government remains accountable to them. It can take forms such as **representative, participatory, deliberative, substantive and elitist democracy**. These models mainly differ over how much direct role ordinary citizens actually have in exercising political power.

### Key ideas of Elitist Theory of Democracy

**1. Rule by a minority:**  
Elitist theory argues that political power is always concentrated in the hands of a **small organised minority**. **Mosca** divides society into a ruling minority and a ruled majority, while **Pareto** speaks of the continuous **circulation of elites**. Thus, one elite may replace another, but elite rule itself continues.

**2. Democracy as competition among elites:**  
Elitist democracy treats elections as the main role available to ordinary citizens. People choose between competing leaders, but actual political decisions are largely made by political elites. **Schumpeter** therefore gives democracy a mainly procedural meaning rather than continuous popular rule.

### Why does Elitist Theory deny democracy as ‘rule of the people’?

**1. Mass rule is considered impractical:**  
Elitists argue that modern societies are too large and complex for every citizen to participate directly in governance. Organised minorities possess greater leadership, knowledge and organisational capacity than the wider population. Hence, power naturally moves towards a relatively small group of decision-makers.

**2. Citizens mainly select rulers:**  
In this view, citizens do not themselves govern; they mainly **choose who will govern them through elections**. Their role becomes periodic rather than continuous. Therefore, democracy becomes rule **with the consent of the people**, rather than literal rule **by the people**.

### How does Elitist Theory deny ‘rule of the people’?

**1. Organisation produces oligarchy:**  
**Robert Michels’ “iron law of oligarchy”** argues that even democratic parties and organisations gradually concentrate decision-making in their leadership. Ordinary members cannot continuously control complex organisations. Thus, institutions created for democracy can themselves develop an elite structure.

**2. Elections may legitimise elite rule:**  
Elitist theory argues that elections often decide **which elite will govern**, rather than transferring real power directly to citizens. In Pareto’s sense, politics becomes circulation of elites rather than disappearance of elites. Democratic institutions may therefore legitimise elite leadership more than establish direct popular control.

### Criticism

**1. Robert Dahl – power is more dispersed:**  
**Robert Dahl** criticises the view that power is concentrated in only one elite. His idea of **polyarchy** holds that power is spread among numerous organised groups and associations. Citizens can therefore exercise influence collectively even when they do not govern directly as individuals.

**2. Macpherson – democracy cannot end with elections:**  
**C. B. Macpherson** criticises democracy reduced merely to procedural competition and elections because it neglects its **substantive and developmental aspects**. Greater participation and developmental opportunities can give ordinary citizens a more meaningful role. Thus, elite dominance is not necessarily the complete meaning of democracy.

### Synthesis

Elitist theory correctly draws attention to the gap between the ideal of **popular sovereignty and the reality of organised leadership**. However, it becomes too deterministic if it assumes citizens can never exercise meaningful influence through participation, civil society, deliberation and accountability.

### Relevant Example

**Political parties** illustrate Michels’ argument well. Although a party may contain thousands or millions of members, everyday strategy and major decisions are generally handled by a much smaller leadership group. This demonstrates how organisation can naturally create a distinction between **leaders and ordinary members**.

### Conclusion

Elitist theory changes democracy from **“rule by the people” to selection and control of competing elites by the people**  
Yet democratic participation and accountability can reduce the distance between **elite leadership and popular sovereignty**.
