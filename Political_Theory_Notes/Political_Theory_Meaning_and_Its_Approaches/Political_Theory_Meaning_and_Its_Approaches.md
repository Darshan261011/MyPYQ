# Political Theory: Meaning and Its Approaches

## Q1. Discuss the Significance of Normative Approach to Political Theory — 2020 (15 Marks)

### What is the Normative Approach?

The **normative approach** studies **what political life ought to be**, rather than merely describing what already exists. It deals with ethical and moral questions concerning **justice, liberty, rights, equality and the good society**. Thus, it provides standards for judging political institutions and practices.

Normative theory uses **reason and value-based arguments** to imagine a desirable political order. For example, **John Rawls** proposed *justice as fairness*, while **Robert Nozick** gave greater importance to individual liberty and a minimal state. Thus, even within normative theory, different visions of a just society are possible.

### Significance of the Normative Approach

First, it provides an **ethical standard to evaluate political power and institutions**. It asks whether authority is legitimate, whether rights are protected and whether social arrangements are just. Therefore, it prevents political theory from becoming merely a description of existing power.

Second, normative theory gives **direction to political action, law and public policy**. Questions such as the common good, social justice and citizens’ rights and duties cannot be answered only through statistics. Normative principles help society decide **what goals political institutions should pursue**.

Third, it provides a basis for **criticism and reform of existing society**. Ideas of justice, equality and rights allow us to question inequality, discrimination or unjust institutions and imagine better alternatives. Hence, normative theory remains important in debates over **democracy, human rights and inequality**.

### Criticism

However, **behaviouralists and realists** argue that normative theory may become too concerned with **“what ought to be”** and ignore how politics actually works. Power struggles, competing interests and practical constraints may make ideal prescriptions difficult to apply. Thus, normative theory can sometimes become overly abstract.

A second problem is that there may be **no single agreement on what is just or good**. For example, **Michael Sandel** criticises Rawlsian liberal individualism and stresses community and social belonging. This shows that normative principles themselves can be contested because different theories begin from different moral assumptions.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Therefore, normative and empirical approaches should **complement each other**. For example, in regulating **AI in public decision-making**, empirical research can tell us whether an algorithm is accurate, but normative theory asks whether its use is **fair, transparent, rights-respecting and accountable**. Facts tell us **what is**; normative theory helps decide **what ought to be**.

### Conclusion

The normative approach gives political theory its **ethical purpose and standards of justice**.  
Its greatest value lies in guiding political reality while remaining informed by **empirical facts and practical conditions**.

## Q2. Difference between Normative and Empirical Theories of Politics

### 1. Basic Question  
**Normative theory** asks **“what ought to be?”** and thinks about how politics should ideally function. **Empirical theory** asks **“what is?”** and studies politics as it actually exists.

### 2. Main Focus  
Normative theory focuses on **justice, liberty, equality, rights and the good life**. Empirical theory focuses on **actual political behaviour, institutions, elections and political processes**.

### 3. Nature of Study  
Normative theory is mainly **prescriptive**, because it suggests what should be done. Empirical theory is mainly **descriptive and explanatory**, because it explains what is happening and why.
### 4. Method  
Normative theory uses **philosophical reasoning and ethical arguments**. Empirical theory uses **observation, surveys, data collection, statistics and hypothesis testing**.

### 5. Role of Values  
Normative theory openly deals with **values and moral judgments**. Empirical theory tries to study politics through **facts and evidence** and generally aims at greater objectivity.

### 6. Verification  
Normative ideas cannot always be tested scientifically because concepts like **justice or fairness** involve value judgments. Empirical theories are expected to be **tested and verified through observable evidence**.

### 7. Example  
**John Rawls** is a good example of normative theory because he asks what principles can create a **just and fair society**. **Robert Putnam**, through *Making Democracy Work*, studied actual democratic performance and civic engagement, making his approach empirical.

### Synthesis  
Both approaches are **complementary**. For example, in democracy, empirical theory can study **voter turnout and voting behaviour**, while normative theory asks whether **voting rights, fairness and democratic duties** are properly protected.

### Conclusion  
Thus, normative theory tells us **how politics should be**, while empirical theory tells us **how politics actually works**.  
Together, they give a more complete understanding of political life.

## Q3. Examine the significance of the Behavioural Revolution in Politics

### What is the Behavioural Revolution?

The **Behavioural Revolution of the 1950s–60s** changed political science from mainly philosophical and institutional study towards the **scientific study of actual political behaviour**. It focused more on what people really do in politics rather than only on constitutions or political ideals.

Behaviouralists such as **David Easton, Robert Dahl and Gabriel Almond** wanted political science to become more empirical and systematic. They therefore emphasised **observation, surveys, data, quantification and verification** in studying political life.

### Significance of the Behavioural Revolution

First, it made political science more **scientific and systematic**. Surveys, opinion polls, statistical analysis and measurable data began to be used to develop theories based on evidence rather than only philosophical speculation.

Second, it shifted attention from only **formal institutions to actual political behaviour**. Studies of voting showed that choices may be shaped by socio-economic background, media and psychological factors; *The American Voter* also highlighted the importance of **party identification**.

Third, behaviouralism widened the study of politics through **comparative and interdisciplinary analysis**. For example, **Almond and Verba's *The Civic Culture*** studied how different political cultures influence the functioning of democracy, while Easton's systems approach examined political interactions through inputs and outputs.

### Criticism

However, behaviouralism sometimes became too concerned with **technique and quantification**. **Alfred Cobban** criticised that technique could become an end in itself, while questions of justice, rights and values received less attention. 

It could also over-focus on individual behaviour and underplay **institutions, history and wider social structures**. **Giovanni Sartori**, for example, argued that behavioural analysis could overlook important structural and institutional forces shaping political outcomes.

### Synthesis

Hence, behaviouralism is most useful when **empirical analysis is combined with normative concerns**. For example, contemporary election studies can use surveys, turnout data and voter-behaviour research to understand how people vote, while questions of **fairness, representation and democratic rights** still require normative political theory.

### Conclusion

Thus, the behavioural revolution made political science more **empirical, systematic and behaviour-oriented**.  
Its lasting value lies in combining scientific methods with attention to the wider **values and institutions of political life**.

## Q4. Decline of Behavioural Revolution

### What is the Behavioural Revolution?

The **Behavioural Revolution of the 1950s–60s** sought to make political science more **scientific, empirical and value-free**. It shifted attention from constitutions and philosophical ideas towards **actual political behaviour**, using observation, surveys, data and quantitative methods.

### What led to the decline of the Behavioural Revolution?

### 1. Neglect of Values and Social Problems  
Behaviouralism concentrated on **facts and value-neutral research**, but politics also involves justice, rights and equality. During the social unrest of the 1960s, especially civil-rights and anti-war movements, behavioural research appeared unable to provide answers to urgent social problems.

### 2. Technique Became More Important than Purpose  
Behaviouralists gave great importance to **measurement, statistics and scientific techniques**. **Alfred Cobban** criticised this tendency by arguing that **technique became an end in itself**; political scientists became more concerned with perfecting methods than solving political problems.

### 3. Excessive Scientism and Loss of Relevance  
The attempt to make political science like a natural science made it increasingly **abstract and detached from political reality**. **David Easton** himself argued that excessive empiricism reduced social relevance, leading him to call for **relevance and action** through post-behaviouralism.

### Synthesis

The decline of behaviouralism did not mean rejection of empirical methods; rather, it produced a **balance between facts and values**. For example, today political scientists can use data to study **social-media voting behaviour**, but understanding issues such as misinformation, fairness and democratic rights also requires normative analysis. This reflects the post-behavioural idea of combining **scientific method with social relevance**.

### Conclusion

Thus, behaviouralism declined mainly because its **scientific methods became disconnected from values and real social problems**.  
Post-behaviouralism retained its empirical strengths while bringing back **relevance, values and action**.

## Q5. Comment on: The Post-Behavioural Approach (2016) – 10 Marks

### What is the Post-Behavioural Approach?

The **Post-Behavioural Approach** emerged in the late 1960s as a correction to the limitations of behaviouralism. It argued that political science should not only study facts scientifically, but should also deal with **values, social problems and human concerns**.

**David Easton** became its major exponent and gave the **“Credo of Relevance.”** He argued that research should be guided by **relevance and action**; therefore, technique is important, but the purpose for which it is used is more important.

### Why did the Post-Behavioural Approach emerge?

Behaviouralism had become excessively concerned with **data, measurement and value-neutrality**. During the civil-rights movements and anti-war protests of the 1960s, political science appeared unable to respond to questions of **justice, rights and social crisis**.

Secondly, behavioural scholars were criticised for remaining in **“ivory towers”** while perfecting scientific techniques. Post-behaviouralism therefore brought back **values and social responsibility**, while still retaining useful empirical methods rather than rejecting science completely.

### Synthesis

Thus, post-behaviouralism seeks a **balance between facts and values**. For example, while studying **misinformation on social media**, empirical research can measure its spread and effects, while normative analysis is needed to address **free speech, fairness and democratic responsibility**. This reflects Easton’s idea that political science must be both **scientific and socially relevant**.

### Conclusion

Post-behaviouralism therefore did not destroy behaviouralism; it **corrected and broadened it**.  
It combined **scientific rigour with relevance, values and action**.

## Q6. ‘Credo of Relevance’ in Post-Behaviouralism Advocates the Importance of Action Science. Analyze. (2023) – 15 Marks

### What is the Post-Behavioural Approach?

**Post-behaviouralism** emerged in the late 1960s as a correction to the limitations of behaviouralism. It tried to bring back **values, ethical concerns and relevance to real social problems**, while still retaining useful scientific methods.

Its main exponent was **David Easton**, who gave the idea of the **“Credo of Relevance.”** He argued that political science should combine **empirical research, normative values, policy relevance and action**, rather than remain only an academic exercise.

### Why did the Post-Behavioural Approach emerge?

First, behaviouralism had become too focused on **statistics, data and quantitative methods**. Political science was becoming technically strong, but it was losing connection with questions of **justice, rights and social problems**.

Second, the **civil-rights movements and anti-war protests** showed that political scientists could not remain neutral observers when society faced serious conflicts. Political science needed to respond directly to such real-world problems.

Third, Easton argued that **technique should serve a purpose**. Research should therefore not only explain political reality but should also help improve it through **relevance, responsibility and action**.

### How does Post-Behaviouralism advocate Action Science?

The **Credo of Relevance** says that knowledge should lead to **action**. Political scientists should not remain detached observers; their research should help address problems such as **social injustice, inequality and other critical social issues**.

Thus, **action science** connects theory with practice through **problem-solving, policy analysis and collaboration between researchers and practitioners**. The aim is not merely to produce knowledge, but to use that knowledge to bring meaningful improvement in society.

### Synthesis

For example, while studying **urban poverty**, empirical research can identify who is excluded from welfare benefits and why. Action science goes a step further by using this evidence to **improve policy design and implementation**, while normative values ensure that the reform remains guided by justice and equality.

### Conclusion

Thus, the **Credo of Relevance bridges the gap between knowledge and action**.  
Post-behaviouralism makes political science both **scientifically sound and socially meaningful**.

## Q7. Comment on: Decline of Political Theory — 2018 (10 Marks)

### What is Political Theory?

Political theory studies the **ideas and values behind political life**, such as justice, rights, power and democracy.  
It does not merely study **what exists**, but also asks **what ought to exist** in a good political order.  
Thus, it gives a normative and philosophical direction to the study of politics.

### Why did Political Theory decline?

**First decline – limitations of traditional political theory:** Traditional approaches became too **philosophical, historical and institution-centred**. They could not adequately explain the rise of **fascism, authoritarianism and Third World politics**, where constitutional text was often different from actual political practice. **David Easton** criticised such historicism and arm-chair theorising for making political theory less relevant.

**Behaviouralism emerged as a response:** After the Second World War, behaviouralists tried to make political science a **scientific discipline** through observation, measurement, verification and value-free analysis. Political science therefore shifted from questions of **“what ought to be” towards “what is.”** However, this gradually pushed normative questions such as justice and morality to the margins.

**Second decline – limitations of behaviouralism:** During the social unrest of the 1950s–60s, behaviouralism could study and measure political behaviour but could not provide answers to questions of **justice, rights and social change**. **Alfred Cobban** argued that technique had become an end in itself, while Easton also recognised the excessive emphasis on empirical methods. Thus, political theory faced a second decline because political science became **too technical and less socially relevant**.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Thus, political theory declines when it becomes **either purely abstract or purely technical**. For example, in **AI-based public decision-making**, data may tell us patterns and outcomes, but it cannot by itself decide what is **fair, just or respectful of privacy and rights**. Hence, political science requires both **empirical facts and normative values**.

### Conclusion

The decline of political theory was therefore **not its death, but a crisis of method and relevance**.  
Its revival lies in combining the **scientific strength of behaviouralism with the normative depth of political theory**.

## Q8. Comment on: Resurgence of Political Theory — 2019 (10 Marks)

### What is Political Theory?

Political theory studies the **ideas and values behind political life**, such as justice, liberty, rights, equality and democracy.  
It not only explains **“what is”**, but also asks **“what ought to be.”**  
Thus, it gives moral and normative direction to political life.

### Why did Political Theory decline?

Political theory faced **two phases of decline**. First, traditional theory became too philosophical and historical and could not adequately explain fascism, authoritarianism and Third World politics; later, behaviouralism made political science excessively **scientific, quantitative and value-free**. As a result, questions of justice, rights and morality were pushed aside.

### How did Political Theory resurge?

The limitations of behaviouralism created the first major push for revival through **post-behaviouralism**. **David Easton** argued that political science should restore **values, relevance and action**, rather than merely perfect scientific techniques. Thus, normative concerns again became important along with empirical study.

The resurgence became stronger with **John Rawls’ *A Theory of Justice* (1971)**, which brought distributive justice back to the centre of political theory. At the same time, movements around **civil rights, gender equality and environment** generated new theories such as feminism, post-colonialism and environmentalism. Political theory therefore expanded to newer questions of identity, difference and justice.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

The resurgence shows that **facts alone cannot tell society what ought to be done**. For example, climate science can tell us the level of environmental damage, but questions like **who should bear the cost and what we owe future generations** require ideas of justice and responsibility. Thus, contemporary problems continue to make political theory relevant.

### Conclusion

Political theory therefore did not disappear; it **revived itself by responding to new social and moral questions**.  
Its strength today lies in combining **empirical understanding with normative guidance**.

## Q9. Comment on: “Political theory is not an escape mechanism but an arduous calling.” — John Plamenatz (2014, 10 Marks)

### Why does Plamenatz say this?

Plamenatz argues that political theory should not become an **escape into abstract ideas** away from actual politics. A political theorist has to confront real questions of **power, justice, rights and governance**. Thus, theory should lead to deeper engagement with political reality rather than intellectual isolation.

He calls it an **“arduous calling”** because deciding what is just or desirable is itself a difficult task. It requires intellectual rigour, ethical judgement and engagement with conflicting values and ideologies. Political theory therefore carries the responsibility of connecting **political thought with political action**.

### Criticism

From the **behaviouralist perspective**, too much emphasis on normative thinking can itself make political theory abstract and subjective. **David Easton** initially stressed a more scientific study based on observable political behaviour and empirical evidence. Therefore, political theory must not become only moral prescription without examining actual political reality.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Hence, Plamenatz does not mean that political theory should ignore facts; rather, **facts and values must work together**. For example, in **AI governance**, empirical research can show how algorithms operate, but questions of **fairness, privacy and accountability** require normative political judgement. This makes political theory a demanding but practically relevant activity.

### Conclusion

Political theory is therefore neither **abstract escapism nor mere collection of facts**.  
Its real calling is to **understand political reality critically and guide it towards a better political order**.

## Q10. Comment on: Systems Approach — 2022 (10 Marks)

### What is Systems Approach?

The **Systems Approach**, associated with **David Easton**, studies politics as an interconnected and dynamic system. Society gives **inputs in the form of demands and supports**, which are converted into **outputs like policies and decisions**. These outputs return through **feedback**, helping the political system respond and adapt to its environment.

### Importance of Systems Approach

First, it gives a **holistic understanding of politics** instead of studying institutions separately. It explains how government, society and environment continuously influence each other. Thus, it helps us understand **interdependence, stability and political change**.

Second, the approach made political analysis more **systematic and scientific** and can be applied to governance, comparative politics and policy analysis. Its feedback mechanism explains how political systems learn from their decisions and adapt to new demands. It also became an important starting point for later approaches such as **Almond and Powell’s structural-functional approach**.

### Criticism

However, **Marxists** criticise the Systems Approach as **status-quoist** because its main concern is system maintenance and stability rather than explaining deep conflict and transformation. **J. D. Singer** also argued that systems thinking can create an unfortunate gap between **theory and actual political practice**.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Yet the approach remains useful if stability is studied along with conflict and change. For example, **public demand for stronger data privacy** can be seen as an input, government regulation as an output, and public or institutional responses as feedback which may lead to further policy changes. Thus, Easton’s model helps us understand politics as a **continuous process rather than a one-time decision**.

### Conclusion

The Systems Approach provides a **dynamic and holistic framework** to understand how political systems operate and adapt.  
Though limited by its emphasis on stability, its **input–output–feedback model** remains useful for analysing modern governance.

## Q11. Discuss David Easton’s Model of Systems Analysis — 2015 (15 Marks)

### What is Systems Approach Analysis?

**David Easton** developed systems analysis to study politics as an **open system continuously interacting with its environment**. He defined the political system as a set of interactions through which **values are authoritatively allocated for society**. Thus, politics is understood as a continuous process rather than simply a study of institutions.

In Easton’s model, society sends **demands and supports as inputs** into the political system. The system processes them and produces **policies and decisions as outputs**, whose effects return to society through **feedback**. This feedback creates fresh demands and enables the system to adjust continuously.

### Importance of Systems Approach

First, Easton gave a **holistic understanding of politics** by connecting government with its social environment. Political institutions cannot be understood separately because demands, decisions and public reactions constantly influence one another. Thus, his model explains the **interdependence** of different parts of political life.

Second, the **feedback mechanism** explains how a political system survives and adapts. When policies create satisfaction or dissatisfaction, society sends new responses to the government, which can modify future decisions. Hence, Easton helps explain **adaptation, stability and continuity** of political systems.

Third, the model can also explain how political systems respond to **stress and change** arising from inside or outside society. Because it is a general model, it can be applied to comparative politics, governance, policy analysis and international relations. It also provided a base for later approaches such as **Almond and Powell’s structural-functional analysis**.

### Criticism

However, the model gives excessive importance to **stability and system maintenance**. **Marxists** therefore criticise it as status-quoist because it does not sufficiently explain deep inequalities, conflict and revolutionary change within society.

Further, the model is highly abstract and may reduce complex politics to a simple **input–output process**. It can underplay power relations, ideology and struggles over whose demands actually enter the system. **J. D. Singer** also criticised systems thinking for creating a gap between **theory and political practice**.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Easton’s model is most useful when **feedback and adaptation are combined with an analysis of power and conflict**. For example, concerns over **AI, privacy and algorithmic bias** become public demands; governments respond through regulations, while reactions from citizens, experts and companies provide feedback for further changes. This shows the continuing relevance of **input → output → feedback → adaptation**.

### Conclusion

Easton transformed political analysis by presenting politics as a **dynamic and adaptive system** rather than a collection of isolated institutions.  
Despite its status-quo bias, his model remains a useful starting point for understanding **policy-making, feedback and political change**.

## Q12. Eurocentrism is both the target and the motive force of Postcolonial Political Theory — 2023 (15 Marks)

### What is Eurocentrism?

**Eurocentrism** is the belief that European or Western ideas, culture and historical experience represent the **universal standard** for all societies. It judges non-Western societies through Western ideas of progress, development and civilization. In this process, indigenous knowledge and experiences often become secondary.

### What is Postcolonial Political Theory?

Postcolonial political theory studies how **colonial domination continues to influence politics, culture and knowledge even after colonial rule ends**. It challenges colonial hierarchies and gives importance to voices and knowledge that were historically marginalised. Thus, it tries to understand politics from the experiences of formerly colonised societies.

### How is Eurocentrism the **Target** of Postcolonial Theory?

First, postcolonial theory directly attacks the idea that the **West is the normal or superior model** of civilization. **Edward Said in *Orientalism*** showed how the West constructed the East as the backward and exotic **“Other.”** Such representations helped intellectually justify colonial domination.

Second, it targets the way Eurocentric knowledge **silences non-Western voices**. **Gayatri Spivak**, through *Can the Subaltern Speak?*, showed how dominant discourse often prevents the subaltern from representing themselves. Therefore, postcolonial theory tries to expose these unequal relations of knowledge and representation.

### How is Eurocentrism the **Motive Force** of Postcolonial Theory?

The experience of Eurocentric domination itself motivates postcolonial thinkers to **rewrite history and political knowledge from non-Western perspectives**. It encourages recognition of indigenous knowledge, local forms of governance and traditions of resistance. Thus, opposition to Eurocentrism becomes a source for creating alternative ways of understanding politics.

Further, this critique pushes postcolonial theory beyond past colonialism towards **present global inequalities and neocolonial practices**. It questions whether ideas of development and progress should follow only one Western path. Its larger aim is therefore cultural diversity, intercultural dialogue and a more equal global order.

### Criticism

However, postcolonial theory can itself become too dependent on a simple **West versus non-West** division. **Homi Bhabha’s idea of hybridity** corrects this by showing that colonial interaction produced mixed identities rather than completely separate cultures. Therefore, political theory should challenge Eurocentrism without treating either Western or non-Western cultures as completely pure and fixed.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

The aim, therefore, is not to **reject everything Western**, but to prevent Western experience from becoming the only universal standard. Contemporary efforts to **decolonise university curricula** illustrate this: Western thinkers are not necessarily removed, but African, Asian, indigenous and other intellectual traditions are also brought into the discussion. This reflects the postcolonial search for a more plural understanding of knowledge.

### Conclusion

Thus, Eurocentrism is the **target** because postcolonial theory seeks to expose its hierarchy and bias.  
It is also the **motive force** because resistance to it produces alternative knowledge, identities and visions of a more inclusive political order.

## Q13. Comment on Postmodernism — 2017 (10 Marks)

### What is Postmodernism?

Postmodernism is a critique of the idea that there is **one universal truth, one universal value or one fixed path of progress**. It sees reality as **fragmented, subjective and shaped by language and power**. Thus, it questions many certainties associated with modern Western thought.

### Main ideas of Postmodernism

First, **Jean-François Lyotard** defined postmodernism as **“incredulity towards metanarratives.”** He rejected grand theories that claim to explain the whole of history or society, such as Enlightenment progress or Marxist class history. Instead, postmodernism gives importance to **plural, local and contingent truths**.

Second, **Michel Foucault** argued that **power and knowledge are closely connected**. What society accepts as truth is often shaped by institutions, dominant discourses and power relations. Therefore, knowledge is not always neutral or independent of power.

Third, postmodernism rejects the idea of a single fixed identity or perspective. It opens space for **marginalised voices, alternative narratives and diverse experiences** which were often excluded by dominant theories. In this way, it broadens political theory beyond one universal framework.

### Criticism

However, excessive rejection of universal truth can lead to **relativism**, where it becomes difficult to judge what is right or wrong. **Jürgen Habermas** criticised postmodernism for abandoning the Enlightenment project of reason and human emancipation. Its focus on language and discourse may also neglect material inequality and economic conditions.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Postmodernism is useful in showing that truth and knowledge can be shaped by **different perspectives and centres of power**, but this should not mean that every claim is equally valid. For example, in the age of **social media and AI-generated content**, the same event may be presented through many competing narratives. This makes postmodernism useful for asking **who creates knowledge, whose voice dominates and whose voice is ignored**.

### Conclusion

Postmodernism challenges **fixed truths, grand narratives and dominant forms of knowledge**.  
Its lasting contribution is to bring **plurality, power and marginalised perspectives** into political theory.

## Q14. Explain the Philosophical Approach to the Study of Political Theory — 2025 (10 Marks)

### What is the Philosophical Approach?

The **philosophical approach is one of the oldest approaches** to political theory, associated especially with **Plato**. It studies politics through ideas such as justice, good life and ideal political order. Its central concern is not merely **“what is” but also “what ought to be.”**

### Key Ideas of the Philosophical Approach

First, it is mainly **normative and prescriptive**. It asks what a good society, just state or desirable political order should look like. Thus, political theory is expected to provide **standards for judging existing political institutions**, rather than merely describing them.

Second, it uses **reason and logical argument** to reach political principles. Plato, for instance, used **dialectics** to understand the nature of justice and governance and developed the idea of an ideal state. Hence, philosophical inquiry searches for the deeper meaning and purpose behind political institutions.

Third, the approach sees political theory as a means to **improve human and political life**. Thinkers such as **Berlin, Rawls and Leo Strauss** continued to give importance to questions of values, justice and the good society. Therefore, political science cannot completely separate itself from questions of moral purpose.

### Criticism

However, the philosophical approach may become **too idealistic and detached from political reality**, producing what behaviouralists called “armchair theories.” **David Easton** criticised traditional normative theory for failing to produce sufficiently verifiable explanations and for allowing moral assumptions to dominate analysis. Thus, philosophical reasoning needs empirical understanding to avoid bias and abstraction.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

The philosophical approach remains necessary because empirical facts can tell us **what is happening, but not what ought to be done**. For example, data can show how **AI surveillance** works, but deciding how far the state should use it requires philosophical questions of **liberty, privacy, justice and public good**. Thus, normative reasoning and empirical analysis should complement each other.

### Conclusion

The philosophical approach gives political theory its **ethical direction and vision of a desirable political order**.  
Its real strength lies in combining **normative ideals with the realities of political life**.
