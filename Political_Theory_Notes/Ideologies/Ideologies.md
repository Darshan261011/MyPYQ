## Q1. Discuss Hannah Arendt’s analysis of the role of Ideology in modern totalitarian regimes. (20) (2018)

Hannah Arendt enjoys a rare designation of being a political philosopher in a male dominated discipline.
She is regarded as a heterodox thinker in the sense that her political philosophy doesn’t belong to any systematic philosophy.
Thus, it is often described as thinking without barriers.

Hannah Arendt was a German Jew who personally witnessed the rise and atrocities of Nazism. This experience made her ask how an entire society could accept such extreme domination. In The Origins of Totalitarianism, she therefore studied Nazism and Stalinism as new forms of rule based on terror and ideology.

Secondly, Arendt believed totalitarianism grows when the public sphere disappears and people become isolated or atomised. Such people lose opportunities for discussion and critical thinking and become more open to an ideology that claims to explain everything. Hence, she examined ideology as a major instrument through which totalitarian regimes control society.

### Arendt’s analysis of the role of ideology

First, ideology gives people a single and complete explanation of the world. Nazism explained history through racial superiority, while Stalinism interpreted it through an inevitable historical movement towards communism. Such ideologies leave little space for alternative explanations or independent thinking.

Second, ideology helps justify terror and oppression. Through propaganda and censorship, the regime presents its ideology as truth and creates a fabricated reality; anyone who challenges it can be presented as an enemy. Thus, ideology gives legitimacy to violence and enables the regime to control both public behaviour and private thought.

Third, ideology becomes strongest among isolated and atomised masses who cannot come together to question official claims. Without an active public sphere, people find it difficult to separate truth from propaganda. Therefore, for Arendt, totalitarianism survives not through terror alone, but through terror supported and justified by ideology.

While Arendt gives major importance to ideology and terror in explaining totalitarianism, Laski explains fascism more as a crisis of capitalism and a counter-revolution by elites against the masses. So, Arendt may underplay the economic and class conditions behind totalitarian regimes by focusing too much on ideology.

A good synthesis is that Arendt’s main insight is still useful: ideology becomes dangerous when people lose the freedom to question it and the State controls information, public debate and dissent. A contemporary example is North Korea, where a 2025 UN report documented tight control over information, State propaganda, surveillance and punishment for behaviour that does not conform to official ideology. Thus, ideology becomes most powerful when it is supported by fear, censorship and the absence of an open public sphere.

Arendt shows that totalitarianism becomes powerful when ideology replaces truth and destroys independent thinking. Therefore, protecting pluralism, public debate and critical thinking is essential to prevent such domination.

## Q2. Factors like community, culture and nation weaken the hegemony of neo-liberalism today. Discuss. (20) (2022)

Neo-liberalism argues for rolling back the welfare state and reviving the night-watchman state. It sees excessive taxation and welfare as an intrusion on individual freedom and believes that markets produce better outcomes than government intervention. Thus, the role of the state should be limited while greater space is given to the market.

### How Community weakens neo-liberalism

**1. Community over market:** Neo-liberalism gives importance to the individual and the market, but communities give importance to collective welfare, local livelihoods and shared resources. When market policies damage local economies or social life, communities demand greater control instead of leaving everything to the market.

**2. Local control over resources:** Community movements challenge the control of large corporations over land, food and resources. For example, La Via Campesina promotes food sovereignty, local farming and resistance to land grabbing and seed patents, thereby placing community control above market control.

### How Culture weakens neo-liberalism

**1. Resistance to cultural homogenisation:** Neo-liberal globalization tends to spread similar market values and lifestyles everywhere, but societies continue to protect their local traditions, identities and ways of life. Joseph Stiglitz points to the conflict between globalization and local cultures, which limits the idea of one universal market model.

**2. Revival of local values:** The revival of indigenous knowledge, languages and traditional practices challenges the dominance of purely market-based values. Similarly, the idea of Asian Values gives greater importance to community and culture than to the completely atomistic individual of liberalism.

### How Nation weakens neo-liberalism

**1. Return of national sovereignty:** Neo-liberalism favours free markets and a reduced role for the State, but nationalism demands that the nation should control its own economy. Concerns about national sovereignty, domestic industries and jobs therefore push governments towards a greater role in economic life.

**2. National interest over global market:** When globalization creates inequality or insecurity, people may demand protection of national industries, employment and welfare rather than unrestricted global competition. Thus, nationalism reasserts the State and national interest against the idea that markets should dominate political choices.

One criticism is that community, culture and nation do not completely replace the attraction of neo-liberalism. Supporters argue that free markets, open trade and globalization can still promote growth, innovation and economic integration. So, even where local identities remain strong, countries may continue to follow market-oriented policies.

A second criticism is that culture itself can sometimes support capitalism rather than resist it. The Frankfurt School scholars argue that mass culture and consumerism can make people accept capitalist values and create new wants. Thus, culture is not always a counter-force; it can also become a vehicle through which market values spread and survive.

A better synthesis is that community and culture do not completely reject markets; they force markets to respect local identities and traditions. For example, the EU’s Geographical Indication system now protects more than 3,700 products, preserving local knowledge and traditional production while still allowing them to compete in the market. Thus, neo-liberalism increasingly has to adjust itself to cultural diversity and community interests.

Similarly, nations are not abandoning markets, but are bringing the State back to protect strategic national interests. India’s PLI schemes across 14 sectors use government incentives to strengthen domestic manufacturing while also attracting investment and promoting exports. This shows that the emerging model is neither pure neo-liberalism nor complete protectionism, but a balance between market efficiency and national priorities.

Thus, community, culture and nation have not ended neo-liberalism, but they have reduced its absolute dominance. The emerging order is a balance between markets and local, cultural and national priorities.

## Q3. Political ideology is primarily concerned with the allocation and utilization of power. Comment. (2021) (15)

The term “ideology” was coined by Destutt de Tracy, who called it the “science of ideas.”

Political ideology gives a framework for understanding who should hold power, how power should be distributed, and for what purpose it should be used. Different ideologies therefore give different answers about the proper role of the State, citizens and institutions in exercising power.

First, ideology decides the allocation of power. Liberalism prefers limited and dispersed power to protect individual rights, socialism seeks redistribution of power towards workers and greater equality, while fascism concentrates power in the State or leader. Thus, ideologies differ mainly in who should possess political power.

Second, ideology also decides the utilization of power. Liberalism uses power mainly to protect liberty and rights; socialism uses State power to reduce inequality and achieve social justice; fascism uses concentrated power for national unity and State supremacy. Hence, ideology determines both where power lies and the goals for which it is exercised.

One criticism is that political ideology is not only about who gets power and how power is used. Ideologies also contain ideas about justice, liberty, equality, society and the ideal role of the State. So, reducing ideology only to power makes its meaning too narrow.

Secondly, ideology is also concerned with how power becomes legitimate. Democratic ideologies justify power through consent and accountability, while authoritarian ideologies may justify it through tradition or leadership. Therefore, ideology deals not only with the allocation and use of power, but also with its justification and moral purpose.

ideology not only decides who gets power, but also the purpose for which power should be used. India’s Constitution, for example, asks the State to promote welfare and reduce inequalities through the Directive Principles. Thus, power and political values work together

Modern political systems also show that ideologies are not always pure or rigid. The European Union combines an open market economy with goals of social justice, employment and social protection. This shows that ideology today guides both the distribution of power and the social goals of its use.

Political ideology is therefore closely linked to power, but cannot be reduced to power alone. It also gives power its purpose, values and legitimacy in society.

## Q4. What do you understand by Multiculturalism? Discuss Bhikhu Parekh’s views on Multiculturalism. (2017) (20)

**1. Recognition of diversity:** Multiculturalism means recognising that society contains different religious, ethnic, national and cultural groups. It accepts that these differences should not be erased in the name of one uniform culture.

**2. Equality with cultural identity:** It argues that minorities should enjoy equal citizenship while also preserving their distinct culture. Thus, multiculturalism goes beyond simple tolerance and seeks recognition, participation and protection of cultural differences.

Parekh rejects the idea that one culture can provide values for everyone. According to him, principles of society should emerge through dialogue among different cultures and civilisations, not by imposing the majority culture

Parekh says every person is a product of culture, every culture is internally plural, and every culture has its own idea of the good life. Therefore, no culture can claim complete superiority over others

Parekh supports giving minorities special rights and space to follow their cultural practices, including non-citizen minorities. However, such practices should be accepted only as long as they do not harm society or the security of the State

Susan Moller Okin criticises multiculturalism from a feminist perspective. She argues that giving special rights to cultural or religious groups may protect practices that discriminate against women within those groups. So, cultural protection should not come at the cost of gender justice.

Amartya Sen warns that excessive emphasis on separate group rights can lead to ghettoisation of minorities. Instead of creating integration, special treatment may make minorities appear separate from the wider society and increase suspicion among communities.

Chandran Kukathas points out the problem of “minorities within minorities.” Protecting a cultural group as a whole may ignore weaker individuals or sub-groups inside it, such as LGBT persons. Therefore, multiculturalism must protect individual rights within communities, not just the community itself.

Multiculturalism works best when cultural differences are recognised without weakening common citizenship. Canada, for example, officially promotes multiculturalism while its Charter guarantees equal rights to all citizens. This reflects Parekh’s idea of diversity within a shared political framework

Cultural communities can receive special protection, but such rights should remain within the limits of equality and basic rights. India follows this balance by protecting minority language, culture and educational institutions under Articles 29–30, while all citizens remain under the same constitutional framework

Multiculturalism should allow cultures to interact, learn and evolve, instead of forcing minorities to adopt the majority culture. New Zealand’s refreshed 2026 Māori Language Strategy seeks to strengthen and normalise te reo Māori in everyday public life, showing how cultural identity can be protected within a common national society

Thus, multiculturalism should protect cultural diversity while also safeguarding individual rights and common citizenship. Parekh’s approach therefore seeks unity through dialogue, not uniformity through assimilation.

## Q5. Comment on the view that socialism in the 21st century maybe reborn as anti-capitalism. (2014) (20)

**Socialism emerged in response to the socio-economic inequalities created by the Industrial Revolution and capitalism.** Therefore, it initially developed mainly as an economic doctrine seeking a more equal distribution of wealth and resources.

**Joseph A. Schumpeter**, in *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, defined socialism as a system in which the **means of production are controlled by public authority**, which decides what and how much to produce and how the output is distributed, instead of privately owned firms.

Socialism rejects the **aggregative view of society** and supports an **organic view**, where society is seen as an interconnected whole. Its core value is **socio-economic equality**, and unlike liberals who stress equality of opportunity, socialists generally give greater importance to **equality of outcomes and sharing of surplus**.

Socialism originally emerged against the inequalities created by capitalism, and this problem still remains. According to the World Inequality Report, the top 10% own a very large share of global wealth, showing why socialist criticism of capitalism remains relevant

The 2008 financial crisis, housing problems, student debt and climate concerns created dissatisfaction with the capitalist model. Therefore, socialism reappears as a criticism of capitalism’s inability to ensure economic equality, social welfare and sustainability

Unlike old revolutionary socialism, the new form can work through democratic and parliamentary methods. Figures such as Bernie Sanders popularised demands like universal healthcare and greater social protection, challenging excessive corporate and elite influence

Thomas Piketty argues for progressive socialism where workers get greater rights and a stronger say in the management of companies. Thus, 21st-century socialism may oppose capitalist inequality not necessarily by abolishing markets, but by demanding redistribution, worker participation and stronger welfare

Friedrich Hayek would question a strong anti-capitalist turn because he believed that too much economic planning and state control can reduce efficiency and individual freedom. So, replacing capitalism completely may create new problems instead of solving inequality.

Milton Friedman defended the free market because he believed it promotes economic efficiency, innovation and freedom of choice. Therefore, socialism in the 21st century may need to correct the excesses of capitalism rather than reject the market itself.

Even within socialism, Edward Bernstein rejected the need for violent revolution and supported the parliamentary and evolutionary path. This suggests that modern socialism may develop through reform, class cooperation and welfare policies, rather than through complete anti-capitalism.

Socialism today may work more as a correction to capitalism than as its complete replacement. The European Union combines an internal market with the goal of a social market economy, social justice and social protection. This shows that anti-capitalist concerns like inequality can be addressed within a regulated market system

2. Modern socialism can also appear through greater worker participation rather than revolution. In Germany, employees in larger companies elect representatives to supervisory boards and take part in important company decisions. This closely fits Piketty’s idea of progressive socialism, where workers get a stronger voice within the economy.

Thus, socialism in the 21st century may survive less as a call to abolish capitalism and more as a challenge to its inequalities and excesses. Its relevance lies in combining markets with welfare, redistribution and greater worker participation.

## Q6. Discuss the key features of pre-Marxist socialist theory. (2015) (15)

Pre-Marxist socialists were thinkers who criticised capitalism before Marx developed scientific socialism. Major thinkers include Saint-Simon, Charles Fourier and Robert Owen, who tried to imagine a more equal and cooperative society. Marx and Engels later called them utopian socialists.

**1. Critique of capitalism:** Pre-Marxist socialism developed as a response to inequality, exploitation and concentration of property under early capitalism. These thinkers criticised private ownership of the means of production and wanted wealth to benefit society more equally.

**2. Cooperative alternative:** They believed society could be reorganised through cooperative communities, collective ownership and planned production. Robert Owen, for example, supported communities where workers lived and worked together and shared the benefits of their labour.

**3. Critique of private property:** Pre-Marxist socialists believed that private ownership of the means of production created inequality and exploitation. They therefore supported collective or social control of property so that wealth could benefit everyone more equally.

**4. Moral improvement:** Pre-Marxist socialists believed that changing social and economic conditions could also improve human nature. They wanted cooperation, altruism and communal living to replace competition, greed and individualism.

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels criticised pre-Marxist socialists as “utopian socialists.” They argued that thinkers like Owen and Fourier imagined ideal cooperative societies, but lacked a scientific explanation of how capitalism would actually be transformed.

Secondly, Marx and Engels felt that pre-Marxist socialism did not properly understand class struggle as the force behind social change. It depended more on moral improvement and cooperation, whereas Marx believed that the conflict between classes had to be understood to explain the transition beyond capitalism.

**1. Cooperative model in India:** The continuing strength of Amul, PACS and dairy cooperatives shows that the pre-Marxist idea of cooperation is not merely utopian. India now has over 1 lakh PACS and around 1.4 lakh dairy cooperatives, showing how collective economic organisation can work within a modern economy.

**2. Planned distribution for social need:** Pre-Marxist socialists wanted production and distribution to serve social needs rather than only market forces. India’s National Food Security system, which provides free foodgrains to around 80 crore people, reflects this idea of using public policy to ensure basic needs and reduce inequality.

Pre-Marxist socialism was utopian in method, but its concerns about inequality, cooperation and social need remain relevant. It therefore laid an important foundation for later socialist thought and welfare-oriented policies.

## Q7. Define Socialism. Discuss the salient features of Fabian Socialism. (2017) (15)

**Socialism emerged in response to the socio-economic inequalities created by the Industrial Revolution and capitalism.** Therefore, it initially developed mainly as an economic doctrine seeking a more equal distribution of wealth and resources.

**Joseph A. Schumpeter**, in *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, defined socialism as a system in which the **means of production are controlled by public authority**, which decides what and how much to produce and how the output is distributed, instead of privately owned firms.

### 3 key features of Fabian Socialism

**1. Gradualism:** Fabians rejected sudden revolution and believed socialism should come slowly through democratic and legislative reforms. They wanted to transform capitalism step by step through welfare, education and social reforms.

**2. Education and persuasion:** Fabians believed socialism would succeed when it became common sense in society. Therefore, intellectuals should spread socialist ideas through writings, lectures and public discussion to influence opinion and policy.

**3. Use of democratic institutions:** Unlike revolutionary Marxists, Fabians wanted to achieve socialism through existing political institutions and democracy. They participated in parliamentary politics and influenced the British Labour Party and social reforms.

## Q8. Fascism displays an ambivalent stance towards parliamentary democracy. Explain. (20 Marks) (2023)

Fascism is derived from the word Fascio which means a bundle of rods, symbolising discipline, unity and strength.
The first fascio was set up with Mussolini as its leader. He further stressed that if 19th century was the age of socialism, liberalism and democracy, the 20th century is to be a century of authority, collectivism and the totalitarian state

Parliamentary democracy is a system where political power works through an elected parliament, political competition, debate and accountability. It rests on pluralism, checks and balances, and the acceptance of opposition.

**1. Ideological rejection:** Fascism fundamentally dislikes pluralism, equality, debate and compromise because it sees them as signs of weakness. It instead stresses unity, discipline and national strength.

**2. Leader above parliament:** Fascism believes a charismatic leader expresses the national will better than parliamentary procedures. Therefore, the leader and state are placed above individual rights, opposition and rule-bound politics.

**3. Yet parliament is useful:** Although fascism rejects democratic values, it may still use elections and parliamentary institutions as a route to gain power. Thus, democracy is accepted tactically but rejected ideologically.

**1. Democracy was dismantled after gaining power:** Once established, fascist regimes moved towards a single-party state, suppressed opposition, controlled the media and removed democratic checks and balances. Parliament was therefore used as a means, not accepted as an end.

1. Fascism accepts democracy only as a route, not as a value. It may use elections and parliament to gain legitimacy, but once in power it prefers unity, hierarchy and centralized authority over debate and pluralism. This is why its relationship with parliamentary democracy is ambivalent rather than simply anti-democratic.

2. Fascism grows when democratic institutions exist without a strong democratic culture. Ortega Gasset and Ernst Nolte suggest that democracy without democratic habits can slide towards fascism, while Erich Fromm explains that people may prefer order and security over freedom. Thus, parliamentary democracy can itself become the entry point for authoritarian politics.

3. Fascism transforms mass participation into mass obedience. Arendt and Popper see fascism as the rise of mass politics and totalitarianism, while Mussolini dismissed parliament as a mere “talking shop.” So, fascism does not reject popular mobilisation; it redirects it away from parliamentary deliberation towards leader-centred political mobilisation.

## Q9. Explain Marx’s understanding of Human Alienation. (2016) (15 Marks)

Karl Marx is best known not as a philosopher but as a revolutionary, whose works inspired the foundation of many communist regimes in the twentieth century.

### What is Marx’s understanding of Alienation?

For Young Marx, human beings are naturally creative and social. Through work, a person should express his creativity and develop himself. But under capitalism, work becomes only a means of earning and survival, rather than a creative activity.

Marx explains alienation at different levels. The worker is alienated from the product, because what he produces belongs to the capitalist, and from the process of production, because he has little control over how and why he works. He is also alienated from other workers, as capitalism creates competition instead of cooperation.

Finally, all these lead to alienation from oneself or one’s “species-being.” A person cannot realise his creative and social human potential through his work. Thus, for Marx, capitalism ultimately separates man from his human essence and makes work dehumanising.

### Why did Marx give this concept?

Marx wanted to show that the problem of capitalism is not merely economic exploitation or low wages. A worker may produce more and more, but loses control over his product, work and ultimately himself. Therefore, Marx used alienation to show how capitalism restricts human freedom and human development, making liberation from alienated labour necessary.

### Criticism

Gramsci suggests that Marx gives too much importance to the economic structure. Human domination cannot be understood only through relations of production because culture, ideology and civil society also shape how people think and behave. Thus, alienation may have cultural and ideological causes as well, not merely economic ones.

Similarly, Foucault broadens the understanding of domination beyond economic class. For him, power operates through institutions, knowledge, discourse and everyday social relations. Therefore, even if economic exploitation is reduced, other forms of domination and alienation may continue.

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Nevertheless, Marx’s idea remains highly relevant, but it can be supplemented by later thinkers. For example, an app-based delivery worker may not control the work process, the customer relationship or the conditions of work; ratings and algorithms regulate his work, while competition separates him from other workers. This reflects Marx’s alienation, while thinkers like Gramsci and Foucault help us understand the cultural, technological and power dimensions that Marx alone may not fully explain.

“That Marx still looms so large in our present world is indeed the measure of his greatness."-Hannah Arendt

## Q10. What is the contemporary relevance of Marxism?

Marxism is a social, political, and economic philosophy named after Karl Marx, which examines the effect of capitalism on labor, productivity, and economic development and argues for a worker revolution to overturn capitalism in favor of communism.

### Main ideas of Marxism

Marxism starts from historical materialism. Marx argued that material and economic conditions shape society, politics and ideas. Changes in the mode and relations of production therefore become the main force behind historical change.

Second, Marx saw society mainly through class relations. Under capitalism, the bourgeoisie owns the means of production while workers sell their labour, creating exploitation and class struggle. Therefore, Marx viewed the state and other institutions largely in relation to the dominant economic class.

Finally, capitalism also produces alienation because workers lose control over their work and human potential. Marx believed these contradictions would ultimately create pressure for transformation towards socialism and eventually communism, based on greater equality and collective ownership.

### Why did Marx develop these ideas?

Marx developed Marxism in response to the inequalities created by industrial capitalism. He saw wealth getting concentrated among owners while workers faced exploitation and poor conditions. Therefore, Marx did not merely want to understand capitalism; he wanted to explain its contradictions and find a path towards human freedom and a more equal society.

### Contemporary relevance of Marxism

Marxism remains relevant in explaining economic inequality and concentration of wealth. Thomas Piketty, though not a Marxist, similarly highlights the tendency of wealth and capital to become concentrated. This keeps Marx's concern about inequality between capital and labour relevant even today.

Marx's idea of alienation is also visible in modern forms of work. Gig workers and workers in insecure employment may have little control over their working conditions, income or work process. Thus, Marx's analysis helps us understand present concerns about precarious work, work-life balance and commodification of labour.

Marxism also helps explain the repeated crises and contradictions of capitalism. The 2008 global financial crisis and movements such as Occupy Wall Street revived debates about class inequality and concentration of economic power. Marxist ideas have also been extended to environmental problems through ecological Marxism, which links unlimited accumulation with exploitation of nature.

### Criticism

Karl Popper criticised Marxism for being too deterministic and historicist. Marx appeared to suggest that history would necessarily move from capitalism towards socialism and communism. Popper argued that such broad historical predictions are difficult to scientifically falsify.

Max Weber challenged Marx's excessive emphasis on economic factors. For Weber, social power does not arise only from ownership and class; status, bureaucracy, authority and ideas can also independently shape society. Thus, Marxism may sometimes give an overly economic explanation of complex social realities.

## Q11. Examine the conception of the State in the ideologies of Fascism and Marxism. (2014) (15)

### Introduction

Both Fascism and Marxism reject the liberal idea of a neutral and limited state, but they understand the state very differently. Fascism makes the state supreme and permanent, while Marxism sees it as an instrument of class domination which should ultimately disappear.

### 1. Nature of the State

For Fascism, the state is an end in itself and represents the unity and strength of the nation. Mussolini's idea was: “Everything in the State, nothing outside the State, nothing against the State.” For Marxism, however, the state is not sacred; it is a class institution created because society is divided into conflicting classes.

### 2. Purpose of the State

The Fascist state exists to create national unity, discipline and national greatness. Individual and group interests must therefore serve the nation. In Marxism, the capitalist state mainly protects the interests of the bourgeoisie; Lenin therefore described it as an “organ of class rule.”

### 3. Individual and the State

Fascism places the state above the individual. Rights and liberties can be restricted because the individual derives meaning from the state; as The Doctrine of Fascism states, “the State is absolute, individuals and groups relative.” Marxism, in contrast, ultimately seeks the liberation of human beings from class exploitation, even though a strong proletarian state exists during the transition.

### 4. Political Organisation

Fascism supports a centralised, authoritarian and single-party state, rejects parliamentary democracy and suppresses political opposition. Marxism proposes a dictatorship of the proletariat after revolution, where workers capture state power and use it to end capitalist class domination.

### 5. Economic Role

Fascism does not seek to abolish capitalism or private property; rather, the state directs economic activity in the larger national interest. Marxism seeks to abolish private ownership of the means of production and move towards collective ownership, removing the economic basis of class divisions.

### 6. Nationalism vs Internationalism

Fascism is based on ultra-nationalism; national solidarity is placed above class divisions and may lead towards militarism and expansionism. Marxism stresses working-class internationalism—workers across countries share common class interests rather than being divided primarily by nationality.

### 7. Ultimate Destiny of the State

This is the sharpest difference. Fascism wants the state to become permanently powerful and all-encompassing. Marxism sees the state as historically temporary: once private property and classes disappear under communism, the state should eventually “wither away.”

### Synthesis + Contemporary Example

Thus, both may favour a powerful state at a particular stage, but for completely different reasons: Fascism glorifies state power itself, whereas Marxism uses state power as a means to eventually create a classless and stateless society.

A useful contemporary illustration is China—not as an example of Fascism, but to test Marx's expectation about the state. China's Constitution continues to define the country as a socialist state and identifies Communist Party leadership as a defining feature of its socialist system. This shows that in actual Marxist-inspired political practice, the state can remain institutionally important for a long period rather than quickly “withering away.”

## Q12. Discuss the relationship between base and superstructure in Marxist theory.

Marxism is a social, political, and economic philosophy named after Karl Marx, which examines the effect of capitalism on labor, productivity, and economic development and argues for a worker revolution to overturn capitalism in favor of communism.

### Relationship between Base and Superstructure

**1. Economic base:** For Marx, the base is the economic structure of society, consisting of the means of production, relations of production and class relations. The basic reason for social organisation is production. Hence, the economy forms the foundation of society.

**2. Base shapes superstructure:** The superstructure includes the state, law, family, education, religion and other socio-political institutions. Marx argues that these institutions largely reflect the economic base. Thus, the class controlling the means of production also gains influence over the institutions of the superstructure.

**3. Superstructure maintains the base:** The superstructure does not merely reflect the economic system; it also helps protect and legitimise the existing class structure. Institutions such as the state, education and religion can make the existing social order appear natural and acceptable. In this way, the superstructure helps maintain the economic base.

**4. Dialectical relationship:** However, the relationship is not completely one-way. The superstructure can also react back upon the economic base, either strengthening it or contributing to its transformation. Therefore, changes and contradictions in one can gradually produce changes in the other.

### Criticism

Gramsci argued that Marx overemphasised the economic base and underestimated the role of culture and superstructure. Similarly, Althusser criticised such an approach as economic reductionism and gave a more multi-structural explanation. Thus, economic relations are important, but politics, culture and ideology also have some autonomy.

### Synthesis – 1

Thus, the base may provide the material foundation, while the superstructure provides legitimacy and stability to that economic system. For example, in today's digital capitalism, large technology markets form an important economic structure, while advertising and digital media culture encourage consumerism, helping the same economic system reproduce itself.

### Synthesis – 2

At the same time, the superstructure can also reshape the economic base, showing that the relationship is two-way. For example, labour laws, privacy regulations or rules protecting platform workers can force companies to change their employment and business practices. Hence, base and superstructure are better understood as interacting rather than mechanically determining each other.

Marxism as an ideology has been so influential that it had divided the world into two blocs. Until class divide, socio economic inequality remains relevant, Marxism would remain relevant.
