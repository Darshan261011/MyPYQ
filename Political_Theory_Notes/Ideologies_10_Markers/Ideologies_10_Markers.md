## Q1. Comment on, “Nothing against the state nothing over it nothing beyond it -Mussolini.” (2018) (10)

Fascism is derived from the word Fascio which means a bundle of rods, symbolising discipline, unity and strength.

“Nothing against the State” means no individual or group can oppose it; “nothing over the State” means no authority is superior to it; and “nothing beyond the State” means no sphere of life is completely outside its control. Thus, Mussolini’s statement expresses the totalitarian character of Fascism.

Mussolini says this because Fascism places the State above the individual. The State is expected to create unity, discipline, order and national strength, while the individual is expected to obey and sacrifice personal interests for the nation. Mussolini’s own fascist doctrine similarly described the State as absolute, with individuals and groups understood only in relation to it

Secondly, Mussolini was rejecting the liberal idea of individual rights, limited government and democracy. Fascism believes that too much individual freedom can weaken collective unity; therefore it supports single-party rule, censorship, absolute obedience and strong authority. This appears in the idea that the fascist state can demand complete obligations from citizens and allows no opposition.

Arendt and Popper criticize Fascism as a form of totalitarianism because making the State supreme destroys individual freedom, rights and political opposition. Gandhi also saw Fascism as a form of Machiavellian politics, where wrong means are justified for political ends. Thus, giving unlimited power to the State can easily turn authority into oppression

The State is necessary for unity, order and collective welfare, but it cannot become absolute. A strong state must therefore remain limited by individual rights, rule of law and accountability

## Q2. Mention the difference between Italian and German brands of fascism. (10) (2025)

Fascism is derived from the word Fascio which means a bundle of rods, symbolising discipline, unity and strength.

### 1. State vs Race

Mussolini’s Italian Fascism made the State supreme—the individual existed for the State. Hitler’s German Nazism made the Aryan race or Volk the central idea; even the State was meant to protect and strengthen the racial community.

### 2. Political Structure

Under Mussolini, Italy still formally retained the monarchy; King Victor Emmanuel III remained the titular head even though Mussolini dominated government. In Nazi Germany, Hitler gradually brought political parties and institutions under Nazi control and established a one-party Führer state.

### 3. Economic Organisation

Italian Fascism gave special importance to the corporate state, where employers and workers were organised into state-controlled corporations. Hitler’s Germany focused more directly on rearmament, autarky and preparation for war, especially through the Four-Year Plan.

### 4. Nature of Expansion

Mussolini’s expansion was mainly aimed at building Italian imperial power and prestige, as seen in the conquest of Ethiopia. Hitler’s expansion was linked to the idea of Lebensraum, seeking territorial expansion mainly in Eastern Europe for the German nation.

Though Italian Fascism and German Nazism differed in emphasis, both rejected liberal democracy, individual rights and political pluralism, and supported authoritarian leadership, nationalism and militarism

## Q3. Discuss Marx’s concept of class (10) (2020)

Karl Marx is best known not as a philosopher but as a revolutionary, whose works inspired the foundation of many communist regimes in the twentieth century.

For Marx, a class is mainly determined by a person’s relation to the means of production. In capitalism, this creates two major classes: the bourgeoisie, who own land, factories and capital, and the proletariat, who do not own these and therefore have to sell their labour. Because the bourgeoisie controls the economic base, it also gains influence over the political and social superstructure.

Marx used the concept of class to explain why inequality and conflict continue in capitalist society. In his view, those who control the means of production can exploit those who depend on wages, creating a basic conflict between bourgeoisie and proletariat. Therefore, he saw class struggle as the driving force of history, eventually pushing workers toward collective action and a classless society.

A major criticism is that Marx reduces society too much to economic class conflict. Society cannot always be divided sharply into only bourgeoisie and proletariat, because in reality there are many middle and mixed classes, and history also shows class cooperation, not only class struggle. Marx has therefore been criticised as an economic reductionist, since factors like culture, politics and ideas can also shape society.

Marx’s concept of class is still relevant because economic inequality, concentration of wealth and unequal power between owners and workers continue today.  The gig economy, precarious employment and movements like Occupy Wall Street against the “1%”, which show that class-based inequalities have not disappeared. At the same time, modern society has many middle and mixed classes, so Marx’s strict bourgeoisie–proletariat division is too simple. Thus, his class theory remains a useful tool to understand inequality and power, but it needs to be applied in a more flexible way to present-day society.

## Q4. Comment on: “Revolution in Permanence” (2022) (10 marks)

Revolution in Permanence is a Marxist concept given by Leon Trotsky

Trotsky gave this concept partly as a critique of the Soviet direction under Stalin. He believed that the Soviet Union was moving away from its revolutionary purpose and becoming increasingly bureaucratic. Therefore, instead of consolidating socialism within one state, Trotsky stressed continuous revolution and international solidarity so that socialism could survive and expand beyond national boundaries.

Trotsky’s “Revolution in Permanence” means that revolution should not stop after one successful political change. The working class must continue the revolutionary process until deeper social and economic inequalities are removed and socialism is established. So, revolution is seen as a continuous process, not a one-time event.

Secondly, Trotsky argued that socialist revolution cannot remain limited to one country. It should spread internationally because capitalism itself operates beyond national boundaries. Ernest Mandel also interprets Trotsky as calling for continuous revolutionary movements against the capitalist order with the aim of achieving global socialism.

## Q5. MK Gandhi’s concept of Swaraj. Comment. (10 Marks) (2019)

MK Gandhi was a lawyer, a freedom fighter and one of the most defining personalities of the twentieth century.

Gandhi believed that freedom from British rule alone would not make India truly free. Political independence would remain incomplete if individuals were still controlled by their desires and society continued with untouchability, communalism and economic dependence. Therefore, he gave Swaraj as a wider idea of freedom involving both self-rule of the country and self-control of the individual.

Swaraj also has a collective dimension. Politically, it means decentralised self-government through Panchayati Raj; socially, ending communalism and untouchability; economically, promoting land reforms, Khadi and cottage industries; and culturally, respecting every culture. Thus, Gandhi’s Swaraj means not merely replacing British rulers with Indian rulers, but creating a self-reliant and morally responsible society.

Gandhi’s Swaraj need not mean going back to isolated villages; its core idea can be adapted as decentralised, participatory and self-reliant governance. A good contemporary example is eGramSwaraj, through which Gram Panchayats prepare development plans, track works and finances, and involve citizens in local decision-making. Thus, modern technology can strengthen Gandhi’s idea of local self-rule rather than replace it.

## Q6. Gandhi’s views on State. Comment (10 Marks) (2015)

MK Gandhi was a lawyer, a freedom fighter and one of the most defining personalities of the twentieth century.

Gandhi was suspicious of a powerful and centralized State because the State finally depends on coercion and force, which conflicts with his principle of Ahimsa. He believed excessive State power can weaken individual freedom and moral responsibility. Therefore, he preferred a minimal State, where people govern themselves as far as possible through Swaraj rather than depending constantly on authority.

His ideal was Ram Rajya and Gram Swaraj—a decentralized system in which power is spread among self-governing village communities instead of being concentrated at the centre. The State should mainly be service-oriented, ensuring justice and the welfare of the weakest, while people manage local affairs through participation and cooperation. Thus, Gandhi did not completely reject the State; he wanted its coercive role reduced and its moral, welfare and enabling role strengthened.

A major criticism is that Gandhi’s idea of a minimal and highly decentralised State may be too idealistic for a complex modern society

Gandhi’s idea of the State can be understood today not as the absence of the State, but as a balance between necessary State capacity and maximum local self-government. The State may handle larger functions like security, welfare and national coordination, while local communities should have real power over everyday development decisions. Kerala’s People’s Planning model is a good example, where local bodies and Gram/Ward Sabhas participate in identifying needs, setting priorities and preparing development plans. This reflects Gandhi’s idea that democracy becomes stronger when power moves closer to the people
