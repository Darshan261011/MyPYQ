## Q1. Comment on Original Position. (2013) — 10 Marks

### What is Original Position?

**Original Position** is a hypothetical situation given by **John Rawls** in *A Theory of Justice*. Here, rational and mutually disinterested individuals choose principles of justice without knowing their own social position, class, talents or advantages. This ignorance is created through the **veil of ignorance**.

### Why did Rawls give this concept?

Rawls wanted the principles of justice to be chosen **fairly and impartially**, without anyone designing rules for his own benefit. If nobody knows whether they will be rich or poor, powerful or weak, they are more likely to choose rules acceptable to everyone.

### Key idea

From the Original Position, Rawls argues that people would choose **equal basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity and the Difference Principle**. They would also follow the **maximin logic**—protecting themselves against the possibility of ending up among the least advantaged.

### Criticism

However, **Michael Sandel** argues that Rawls imagines an overly abstract individual. In reality, people are **“embedded selves”** shaped by family, community, culture and social relations; therefore, they cannot completely detach themselves from these identities while deciding what is just.

### Synthesis

Still, the Original Position is useful not as a description of real political decision-making, but as a **thought experiment for impartiality**. It asks us to judge institutions without first knowing whether we personally gain or lose from them.

### Relevant example

Suppose people are designing an **education policy** without knowing whether they will be born into a rich or poor family. They would likely support equal access to basic education and additional support for disadvantaged children, because they themselves might later occupy that position.

### Conclusion

Thus, the Original Position is the foundation of Rawls’s idea of **“justice as fairness.”**  
Its strength lies in making justice impartial, though critics question whether individuals can ever be completely separated from their social context.

## Q2. Comment on Distributive Justice. (2018) — 10 Marks

### What is Distributive Justice?

**Distributive justice** is concerned with the fair distribution of **resources, wealth, opportunities and social benefits** among members of society. It asks who should receive what, on what basis, and how inequalities should be treated.

### How is Rawls’ idea of justice distributive?

For **John Rawls**, justice requires **equal basic liberties and fair equality of opportunity**. However, he does not demand complete economic equality; inequalities can exist if they improve the position of the **least advantaged**.

This is his **Difference Principle**. Rawls therefore focuses on how society’s primary goods and opportunities should be distributed so that natural or social disadvantages do not decide a person’s entire life chances. Thus, his *justice as fairness* is essentially distributive in character.

### Criticism

**Robert Nozick**, in *Anarchy, State and Utopia*, rejects such patterned redistribution. For him, justice depends on whether property was **justly acquired and voluntarily transferred**, and excessive redistribution by the State can violate individual property rights.

### Synthesis

Hence, distributive justice should balance **social equality with individual freedom**. Rawls reminds us to protect the least advantaged, while Nozick reminds us that redistribution must also respect legitimate individual rights.

### Relevant example

A **progressive taxation system** reflects distributive justice: higher-income groups contribute more taxation, while public revenue is used for education, healthcare and welfare for disadvantaged sections. This allows inequality, but seeks to improve the position of those at the bottom.

### Conclusion

Thus, distributive justice is not simply about **equal distribution**, but about finding a fair basis for distributing social benefits and burdens.  
Rawls gives it an egalitarian direction by making the **least advantaged the test of a just distribution**.

## Q3. Comment on Difference Principle in Rawls’ Theory of Justice. (2015) — 10 Marks

### What is the Difference Principle?

The **Difference Principle** is part of John Rawls’ second principle of justice in *A Theory of Justice*. It says that social and economic inequalities are acceptable only when they work to the **greatest benefit of the least advantaged**. Thus, Rawls does not demand complete equality.

### Why did Rawls give this concept?

Rawls recognised that even after equal liberty and opportunity, inequalities will arise because people differ in **talent, ability and social circumstances**. Therefore, instead of eliminating every inequality, he wanted to **manage inequality fairly**. The gains from inequality should also improve the condition of those at the bottom.

### Key idea

The principle combines **liberty with equality**. First, everyone should have equal basic liberties and fair equality of opportunity; after this, inequality can exist only if it benefits the least advantaged. Rawls derives this through the **Original Position and Veil of Ignorance**, where nobody knows what position they will occupy in society.

### Criticism

**Robert Nozick** challenges this redistributive approach in *Anarchy, State and Utopia*. He argues that if property has been acquired and transferred justly, the State should not continuously redistribute it simply to achieve a preferred social pattern. Thus, Rawls may give too much importance to redistribution at the cost of individual entitlement.

### Synthesis

Rawls therefore offers a middle path between **complete economic equality and unrestricted inequality**. Inequality itself is not unjust; what matters is whether the social arrangement also improves the position of those who are worst off.

### Relevant example

Consider **progressive taxation used to finance public education and healthcare**. Higher earners are allowed to retain greater incomes, but part of their contribution supports services for poorer citizens. In Rawlsian terms, such inequality can be justified when it helps improve the position of the **least advantaged**.

### Conclusion

The Difference Principle makes **fairness, rather than absolute equality**, the basis of distributive justice.  
Rawls thus accepts inequality, but only when the **weakest sections also gain from it**.

## Q4. Examine the Entitlement Theory of Justice. (2022) — 15 Marks

### What is Entitlement Theory of Justice?

**Entitlement Theory of Justice** was given by libertarian philosopher **Robert Nozick** in *Anarchy, State and Utopia* (1974). It argues that justice is not about creating an equal pattern of wealth, but about whether a person has **rightfully obtained what he possesses**.

For Nozick, individuals have strong rights over their **life, liberty and property**. Therefore, if property has been acquired and transferred through just means, the resulting distribution remains just even when it produces considerable economic inequality.

### Why did Nozick give this concept?

Nozick developed entitlement theory mainly as an alternative to **Rawls’ redistributive theory of justice**. He believed that continuously redistributing legitimately earned wealth in the name of equality interferes with **individual liberty and property rights**; hence he supported a minimal rather than welfare State.

### Key ideas

**1. Justice in acquisition:** A person is entitled to property when it is acquired through legitimate means without violating the rights of others. Influenced by **Locke**, Nozick gives importance to labour and legitimate acquisition in establishing ownership.

**2. Justice in transfer:** Property can be transferred through **voluntary exchange, sale or gift**. If the original holding is just and the transfer takes place without coercion or fraud, the resulting unequal distribution is also just.

**3. Justice in rectification:** If property was acquired through **force, fraud or another injustice**, the wrong must be corrected. Thus, Nozick does not defend every existing inequality; only holdings produced through a just history of acquisition and transfer are legitimate.

### Criticism

From a Rawlsian perspective, entitlement theory can allow **very large inequalities** even when the least advantaged receive little benefit. By restricting the State mainly to protection against force, theft, fraud and enforcement of contracts, Nozick may give insufficient attention to unequal starting conditions and **social justice**.

### Synthesis

The debate therefore lies between **liberty and social equality**. Nozick reminds us that redistribution should respect individual freedom and legitimate ownership, while Rawls reminds us that a just society must also ensure fair opportunities for those born with social and economic disadvantages.

### Relevant example

Suppose **A earns ₹10 lakh through lawful work and voluntarily pays ₹2 lakh to B for a car**. Even if A remains much richer than B, Nozick would call the resulting distribution just because both the acquisition and transfer were voluntary and legitimate; inequality itself is not injustice.

### Conclusion

Thus, Nozick shifts justice from **“how much everyone has” to “how everyone got what they have.”**  
Entitlement theory strongly protects liberty and property, but its challenge is dealing with **deep inequalities and unequal starting conditions**.

## Q5. How has Rawls enriched the idea of justice in liberalism? (2021) — 20 Marks

### Rawls and the context

**John Rawls** was a social liberal and egalitarian who described justice as the **“first virtue of social institutions.”** His *A Theory of Justice* (1971) brought questions of justice, equality and fairness back to the centre of political philosophy.

Rawls wrote when behavioural political science was being criticised for neglecting normative questions raised by movements for equality and rights. He therefore tried to develop a rational theory of justice that could combine the traditional liberal concern for **liberty with social equality**.

### What is Rawls’ Theory of Justice?

Rawls calls his theory **“justice as fairness.”** He imagines rational individuals in an **Original Position**, choosing principles of justice behind a **Veil of Ignorance**, where they do not know their class, talents or social position. This makes their choice impartial.

Such individuals, according to Rawls, would first choose **equal basic liberty for everyone** and then **fair equality of opportunity**. Liberty therefore remains the first priority and cannot simply be sacrificed for economic equality.

Finally comes the **Difference Principle**: social and economic inequalities may exist only when they benefit the **least advantaged**. Thus, Rawls does not seek to eliminate inequality; he seeks to manage it according to fairness.

### How did Rawls enrich liberal justice?

First, Rawls **reconciled liberty and equality**. Classical liberalism gave greater emphasis to individual liberty and property, whereas Rawls showed that liberalism could protect liberty while also correcting unfair social and economic disadvantages.

Second, he transformed liberal justice from merely **formal equality into substantive fairness**. Equal legal rights are not enough; people must also receive fair opportunities, and inequalities should improve the position of those who are worst off.

Third, Rawls provided a stronger justification for the **welfare orientation of liberalism**. Progressive taxation, redistribution and social welfare can be justified without rejecting individual freedom because inequality itself is allowed, provided it also benefits the least advantaged. His theory therefore gave liberalism a stronger social-justice dimension.

### Criticism

**Robert Nozick** argues that Rawls gives too much importance to redistribution. If property is acquired and transferred justly, redistributing it through progressive taxation can interfere with **individual liberty and entitlement**; Nozick therefore prefers a minimal State.

**Michael Sandel** criticises Rawls’ idea of the individual in the Original Position. He argues that human beings are **“embedded selves”**, shaped by family, culture and community, and cannot be completely separated from these social relationships while deciding what is just.

### Synthesis

Rawls later responded to such concerns through the idea of **overlapping consensus** in *Political Liberalism*. People in a plural society may disagree on religion or morality but can still agree on certain common political principles of justice, making Rawlsian liberalism more sensitive to diversity.

### Relevant example

Consider **progressive taxation used to finance public education and healthcare**. Rawls would not object to some citizens earning much more than others, but the institutions should ensure that part of the social gains also improves the opportunities and living conditions of the **least advantaged**.

### Conclusion

Rawls enriched liberalism by moving it from a narrow defence of **liberty and formal equality towards justice as fairness and democratic equality**.  
His lasting contribution is the attempt to combine **individual freedom, fair opportunity and concern for the least advantaged** within one liberal framework.

## Q6. Examine Communitarian Perspectives on Justice. (2019) — 15 Marks

### What is Communitarian Perspective on Justice?

**Communitarianism** argues that justice cannot be understood by looking at an isolated individual alone. A person is shaped by **family, community, culture and traditions**, so ideas of right and justice also arise from these social relationships.

It developed mainly as a critique of **liberal individualism, especially Rawls**. Communitarians such as **MacIntyre, Sandel, Taylor and Walzer** questioned the idea that individuals can be separated from their social background while deciding universal principles of justice.

### Key ideas of Communitarian Justice

**1. Embedded individual:** **Michael Sandel** argues that a person is an **“embedded self”**, not an isolated individual. Our identity, values and moral obligations are formed through social relationships; hence justice must take the community surrounding the individual into account.

**2. Common good:** Communitarians argue that justice is not merely about distributing rights and resources among individuals. It must also promote the **common good, shared values and collective responsibilities** that hold a community together.

**3. Context-specific justice:** **Michael Walzer** argues that there cannot be one universal principle for distributing every social good. Different communities give different meanings to goods, so justice should recognise cultural context and distribute different goods according to their appropriate social meaning.

### Criticism

Too much emphasis on community may weaken **individual autonomy and rights**. A community may contain traditional practices that disadvantage some of its own members; therefore, community values cannot automatically be treated as just merely because they are socially accepted.

Further, communitarianism faces the problem of **cultural relativism**. If every community has its own conception of justice, it becomes difficult to establish common standards for judging injustice, particularly in diverse societies containing many competing communities.

### Synthesis

A better position is to combine **liberal rights with communitarian social context**. Rawls later moved in this direction through **overlapping consensus**—people with different beliefs and community identities can still agree on some common political principles of justice.

### Relevant example

Consider the distribution of **healthcare and political offices**. Healthcare may reasonably be distributed according to **need**, while political office should follow democratic rules and public responsibility. This supports Walzer’s idea that different social goods cannot always be distributed by one single principle.

### Conclusion

Communitarianism enriches justice by showing that individuals are **socially embedded and justice is shaped by community and shared values**.  
However, a just order must balance the **common good with individual rights and universal minimum standards**.

## Q7. Analyze the Rawlsian justification of discrimination to achieve goals of social justice. (2018) — 15 Marks

### Introduction

**John Rawls**, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), developed the idea of **“justice as fairness.”** He was concerned with reconciling liberty with equality and creating institutions that protect basic freedoms while improving the condition of disadvantaged sections.

### Rawls’ Theory of Justice

Rawls uses the **Original Position and Veil of Ignorance**, where individuals choose principles of justice without knowing their class, status, talents or social background. Since nobody knows where they will finally stand, the principles chosen are expected to be fair and impartial.

From this position, Rawls gives priority to **equal basic liberties**, followed by **fair equality of opportunity** and the **Difference Principle**. Social and economic inequalities are acceptable only when they work to the benefit of the **least advantaged**.

### How does Rawls justify discrimination for social justice?

First, Rawls distinguishes **arbitrary discrimination from justified differential treatment**. Treating people differently merely because of caste, race or gender is unjust; but favourable treatment may be justified when it corrects disadvantages produced by unequal social circumstances.

Second, **fair equality of opportunity** requires more than simply opening the same door to everyone. If some groups begin from deeply unequal social or economic positions, special support may be needed to create a genuine **level playing field**.

Third, the **Difference Principle** provides the stronger justification. Preferential policies can be defended when they improve the position of historically or socially disadvantaged groups and reduce inequalities in access to opportunities, resources and power. Thus, unequal treatment can sometimes promote **substantive equality**.

### Criticism

**Robert Nozick** objects to such redistributive logic because it may interfere with individual liberty and legitimate entitlements. From this view, policies favouring some groups can become unfair to others if they override merit or justly acquired advantages.

**Ronald Dworkin** offers a different criticism: Rawls does not sufficiently distinguish between disadvantage caused by **brute luck** and that caused by individual choice. For Dworkin, compensation is stronger when inequality arises from circumstances beyond a person’s control.

### Synthesis

Rawls therefore does not defend discrimination in its negative sense; he provides a case for **positive discrimination or affirmative action** when it corrects unequal starting conditions. The test is whether such differentiation expands fair opportunity and benefits those who are genuinely disadvantaged.

### Relevant Example

India’s constitutional framework provides a useful parallel. **Articles 15(4) and 16(4)** permit special provisions and reservations for certain socially and educationally backward or inadequately represented classes; this resembles the Rawlsian idea that differential treatment may be used to move towards substantive equality.

### Conclusion

Rawls shows that **formal equal treatment can sometimes preserve real inequality**, while limited differential treatment may promote justice.  
Thus, discrimination is defensible only when it serves **fair opportunity and the least advantaged**, rather than creating arbitrary privilege.

## Q8. Critically examine John Rawls’s argument for Democratic Equality. (2016) — 15 Marks

### Introduction

**John Rawls**, a social liberal and egalitarian, presented his theory of **“justice as fairness”** in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). He tried to construct a society where individual liberty is protected while social and economic inequalities are made fair.

Rawls emerged when purely empirical approaches to politics were being questioned and normative political theory was regaining importance. His work brought **justice, equality and fairness** back to the centre of political philosophy and attempted to reconcile liberty with equality.

### Rawls’ arguments for Democratic Equality

**1. Fair starting point:** Rawls begins with the **Original Position and Veil of Ignorance**. People choose principles of justice without knowing their class, wealth, talents or social position; therefore, nobody can design institutions only for his own advantage.

**2. Liberty with fair opportunity:** Rawls first guarantees **equal basic liberties** to everyone and then demands **fair equality of opportunity**. Thus, democratic equality is not merely equal political rights; social institutions should also give people a genuinely fair chance to compete for positions.

**3. Difference Principle:** Rawls accepts that inequalities will arise because people differ in talents and efforts. However, such inequalities are just only when they work to the **greatest benefit of the least advantaged**. Hence he calls his position democratic equality rather than complete equality of outcome.

### Criticism

**Michael Sandel** argues that Rawls imagines an overly abstract individual. People are **“embedded selves”** shaped by family, community and culture; therefore, justice cannot be decided by completely separating individuals from their social identities.

From a feminist perspective, **Susan Moller Okin** argues that Rawls concentrates mainly on justice in the public sphere while paying insufficient attention to inequalities within the **family and private sphere**. Thus, formal democratic equality may coexist with deeper gender inequalities.

### Synthesis

Rawls’ democratic equality is important because it moves beyond both **formal equality and absolute equality**. It protects equal liberty while allowing inequality only under conditions of fair opportunity and benefit to the weakest sections.

### Relevant Example

Consider **progressive taxation used to provide quality public education**. Higher earners may continue to earn more, but part of the social resources are used to improve opportunities for disadvantaged children. This reflects Rawls’ idea that inequality is acceptable when it also improves the position of the least advantaged.

### Conclusion

Rawls makes democracy more substantive by joining **equal liberty, fair opportunity and concern for the least advantaged**.  
Despite its abstract nature, his idea of democratic equality remains a major attempt to reconcile **liberty with social justice**.

## Q9. Rawls’ idea of ‘liberal self’ is too individualistic. Explain the communitarian critique of Rawls’ theory of Justice. — 15 Marks

### Introduction

**John Rawls**, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), developed **justice as fairness** based on individual liberty, equality of opportunity and fair distribution. His theory assumes a **rational and autonomous individual** capable of deciding and revising his own conception of the good life.

This idea reflects the liberal tradition where the **individual is morally prior to the community**. However, communitarians such as **Sandel, Taylor and MacIntyre** argue that individuals cannot be understood separately from the social and cultural relationships in which they live.

### Why does Rawls require the idea of the Liberal Self?

Rawls needs an **autonomous self** for his Original Position. Individuals should be capable of choosing principles of justice rationally without allowing their religion, class, community or personal interests to influence their judgement.

Therefore, under the **Veil of Ignorance**, individuals are temporarily separated from knowledge of their social identities and advantages. Rawls believes this makes the choice of principles **impartial and fair**, rather than allowing people to design justice for their own group.

### Communitarian Criticism

**Michael Sandel**, in *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice*, rejects this detached individual. He argues that a person is an **“embedded self”** whose identity, values and obligations arise from family, community and social relations; therefore, such ties cannot simply be removed while deciding justice.

Similarly, **Charles Taylor** describes the individual as a **“situated self.”** Human goals and ideas of the good are shaped by shared culture and common values. Hence, Rawls gives too much importance to individual choice while underestimating the community that gives meaning to those choices.

**Alasdair MacIntyre**, in *After Virtue*, argues that moral reasoning itself develops through **traditions and practices of communities**. Thus, there cannot be a completely neutral individual standing outside history and culture and then independently choosing universal principles of justice.

### Synthesis

The communitarian critique does not require abandoning individual rights; rather, it asks liberalism to recognise that **autonomy itself develops within society**. Rawls later moved towards this concern through **Political Liberalism** and the idea of **overlapping consensus**, where people from different communities can agree on common political principles.

### Relevant Example

A person may consider **caring for elderly parents** a moral duty, not simply because of an independent rational choice, but because family and cultural traditions shape that sense of obligation. This illustrates the communitarian claim that our idea of what is right is often formed within social relationships.

### Conclusion

Rawls’ liberal self protects **autonomy and impartiality**, but communitarians show that the individual is never completely detached from society.  
Justice therefore requires a balance between **individual rights and the communities that shape individual identity and moral values**.

## Q10. Make a comparative assessment of Greek Perspective of Justice with the Rawlsian concept of Justice. (2020) — 20 Marks

### Greek Perspective of Justice

The Greek idea of justice is mainly represented by **Plato and Aristotle**. Plato sees justice as **functional specialisation**—each person performs the function suited to his nature—while Aristotle sees justice as proportional equality, i.e. **“treat equals equally and unequals unequally”** according to merit.

### Rawls and Context

**John Rawls**, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), develops **“justice as fairness.”** Through the Original Position and Veil of Ignorance, he tries to establish principles that protect equal liberty, fair opportunity and the interests of the least advantaged.

### Greek Justice vs Rawlsian Justice — 8 Dimensions

**1. Basic idea — Harmony vs Fairness:** For Plato, justice means **harmony**, where every class performs its proper function. For Rawls, justice means **fairness**, where social institutions are arranged according to principles that free and equal persons would accept.

**2. Nature of society — Hierarchical vs Egalitarian:** Greek justice accepts a **hierarchical social order** based on different abilities and functions. Rawls begins from the moral equality of persons and therefore develops a more **egalitarian conception of justice**.

**3. Basis of distribution — Merit vs Least Advantaged:** Aristotle distributes social goods according to **merit and contribution**. Rawls permits inequalities only when they improve the position of the **least advantaged**, through his Difference Principle.

**4. Individual and community — Duty vs Rights:** Greek thinkers give greater importance to the individual's **duty towards the polis and common good**. Rawls gives stronger protection to **individual basic liberties and rights**, which cannot simply be sacrificed for collective welfare.

**5. Method — Teleological vs Contractual:** Greek justice is largely **teleological**—it asks what functions, virtues and social arrangements produce the good life. Rawls follows a **contractual method**, deriving justice from a hypothetical agreement in the Original Position.

**6. View of inequality — Natural vs Socially regulated:** Plato accepts **natural inequality**, believing different people are suited to different social functions. Rawls accepts that inequalities may arise, but insists that institutions must regulate them through fair opportunity and the Difference Principle.

**7. Goal of justice — Good life vs Fair cooperation:** Greek justice aims at creating a **virtuous and harmonious polis** in which citizens can achieve the good life. Rawls is more concerned with establishing a **fair system of social cooperation** among free and equal citizens.

**8. Moral foundation — Virtue vs Human dignity:** Greek justice is closely linked with **virtue, excellence and proper performance of social roles**. Rawls, influenced by Kant, places greater emphasis on the **inviolability and equal moral worth of every individual**.

### Synthesis

Despite these differences, both approaches reject the idea that justice simply means giving everyone exactly the same thing. Greeks stress **proportionate treatment according to function or merit**, while Rawls allows inequalities under fair conditions; both ultimately seek a stable social order, though they define fairness differently.

### Conclusion

Greek justice moves from **virtue, merit and the common good**, whereas Rawls moves from **liberty, equality and fairness**.  
Thus, Rawls transforms the older idea of proportionate justice into a modern democratic concern for **equal dignity and the least advantaged**.
